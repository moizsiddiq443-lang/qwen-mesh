# Why Free Tiers Fail: A Deep Dive into the Unsuitability of Serverless, HF Spaces, and CI Runners for 24/7 Agents

## Serverless GPU Platforms: The Challenge of Ephemeral Compute

The emergence of serverless GPU platforms represents a significant evolution in cloud computing, offering a model designed for scalability and cost-efficiency by executing code only when triggered and scaling down to zero during idle periods [[16]]. This approach eliminates the need for users to manage underlying infrastructure and shifts billing from a fixed, per-hour cost to a pay-per-use model based on actual compute time [[7,8]]. While this paradigm is exceptionally well-suited for asynchronous jobs, background processing, and sporadic user-facing requests, its fundamental architecture presents profound challenges for supporting the sustained, uninterrupted operation required by a 24/7 agent workload. The core trade-off inherent in the serverless model—namely, the mitigation of idle costs through ephemeral compute—is fundamentally misaligned with the persistence demanded by autonomous agents.

A primary architectural barrier for continuous operation is the phenomenon known as a "cold start." When a new request arrives and no worker is available to handle it, the serverless platform must initiate a sequence of actions to bring a computational unit online. This process involves pulling the necessary container image, loading model weights into GPU memory, and initializing the application runtime environment [[16]]. The duration of this process can vary significantly depending on the size of the model and the footprint of the container, ranging from under a second to several seconds [[16]]. For instance, some providers like Runpod claim spin-up times of less than 200ms for many workloads [[9,16]], while others like Azure Container Apps report cold starts of around 5 seconds and Baseten between 8 and 12 seconds [[9]]. Modal, another prominent provider, reports cold starts of approximately 2-4 seconds [[9]]. For an AI agent whose functionality relies on real-time responsiveness or the maintenance of a constant, active state, such delays are often prohibitive. Even if a platform offers mechanisms to mitigate this, such as configuring a minimum number of "warm" instances to always be available—a feature noted for Runpod Serverless [[16]]—this strategy fundamentally undermines the core value proposition of serverless computing. Maintaining these warm instances consumes resources continuously, thereby incurring costs even during periods of inactivity and negating the benefit of scaling to zero.

Furthermore, serverless functions are architected for short, discrete tasks rather than long-running processes. Most serverless frameworks are optimized for execution durations measured in milliseconds to seconds. A single workflow run on GitHub Actions, for example, has a maximum duration of 35 days, but this limit applies to the entire workflow, not an individual job; a single job within that workflow is limited to 6 hours [[10,11]]. Similarly, Modal allows for custom execution timeouts, with one source stating a default of 300 seconds (5 minutes) and another noting that users can specify durations up to 24 hours [[24]]. However, this level of configurability is not standard across all platforms and may be an exception rather than the rule. A long-running agent process, especially one engaged in continuous data collection, state management, or interaction with external services over time, falls outside the design paradigm of most serverless systems. Such a process would likely be terminated by the platform's infrastructure manager as it exceeds the typical execution window for a single function invocation [[16]]. The platform is built to handle bursts of activity, not sustained, linear computation.

Another critical limitation is the inherent statelessness of serverless environments. Any state maintained by an agent within a single function execution, such as variables in memory or open connections, is lost once the function completes. To maintain a persistent session or memory across multiple invocations, the agent must rely on external state management services like Redis or databases. While technically feasible, this adds significant complexity, introduces potential points of failure, and increases latency due to network round-trips to the external store. This architectural constraint makes building a truly persistent agent, one that can learn and adapt over time without losing context, exceedingly difficult and inefficient within a standard serverless framework. The very nature of the serverless execution model, where each invocation is isolated, works against the concept of a continuous, evolving agent intelligence.

From a commercial perspective, the viability of free tiers on these platforms is minimal and primarily intended for experimentation and development. Modal, for example, offers a 'Starter' plan that includes $30 in free credits per month, 100 containers, and 10 GPU concurrency [[7]]. While generous for initial testing, this allowance is quickly exhausted by any non-trivial workload. Running a simple model inference task repeatedly throughout a day would deplete these credits in a matter of hours, let alone a full 24/7 operation. More importantly, the business landscape for pure-play serverless GPU providers is volatile. The recent announcement of Banana's serverless GPU platform being sunsetting serves as a stark reminder of the financial and engineering challenges involved in providing reliable, affordable, and high-performance GPU access at scale [[29]]. This instability implies that free tiers, which represent the smallest revenue-generating segment, are particularly susceptible to being discontinued, heavily restricted, or converted to paid models with little notice.

Finally, the terms of service for these platforms often contain clauses that explicitly disclaim any guarantee of uptime or availability and reserve the right to suspend or terminate a user's service without notice [[30]]. Runpod's terms, for instance, state that the service is provided "on an as-is and as-available basis" and that they can suspend a user's account if they "unduly stress system resources" [[30]]. An agent running continuously, even if highly efficient, could be flagged by the platform's monitoring systems as excessive resource usage, leading to its termination. Furthermore, many platforms prohibit automated use of the system, which could encompass the behavior of a background agent, potentially violating the terms of service and resulting in account suspension [[30]]. These commercial and legal risks make relying on a serverless GPU platform for a mission-critical, 24/7 agent workload a precarious undertaking. In summary, serverless GPU platforms are fundamentally misaligned with the requirements of a persistent, 24/7 agent workload. Their architecture prioritizes bursty, stateless, and ephemeral computation over sustained, stateful, and continuous operation, making them a poor fit for this specific use case.

## Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU: A Highly Constrained Environment

Hugging Face Spaces, particularly with its integrated ZeroGPU feature, presents a seemingly attractive option for deploying machine learning applications due to its tight integration with popular libraries like Gradio and PyTorch. The platform simplifies the process of creating interactive demos and sharing models, which has made it immensely popular in the AI community. However, a detailed examination of ZeroGPU's operational mechanics reveals severe, deliberate limitations that render it fundamentally unsuitable for supporting long-running, autonomous agent workloads. Its design is explicitly tailored for short-lived, interactive inference tasks, not for the persistent execution that defines a 24/7 agent.

The most significant and immediate barrier to using ZeroGPU for a continuous agent is its strict daily time quota system. Unlike other platforms where limits might be based on total monthly usage, ZeroGPU operates on a hard, daily cap that resets every 24 hours after the first usage of that day [[3,4]]. The limits are tiered based on the user's account type:
*   Unauthenticated users are limited to 2 minutes of GPU time per day.
*   Free-tier account holders receive a slightly more generous allocation of 3.5 minutes per day.
*   PRO account holders get a higher limit of 25 minutes per day.

This quota system means that a 24/7 agent cannot operate for more than 3.5 consecutive minutes before being blocked from further GPU execution for the remainder of the day [[3]]. This is a direct and insurmountable obstacle to continuous operation. The quota is separate from the monthly inference credits used by other Hugging Face services like HuggingChat Omni, meaning an agent's inability to run is determined solely by this daily clock, regardless of the user's overall credit balance [[4]]. The quota consumption model further exacerbates this issue. It operates on a reservation-based system where quota is consumed based on the requested reservation duration upon admission, not on the actual time the GPU was used [[1]]. If an application author specifies a `duration` parameter of 60 seconds for a prediction, that 60-second slot is reserved and consumed from the user's quota immediately upon starting the task, even if the actual computation finishes in one second [[1,5]]. This can lead to rapid and unpredictable exhaustion of the already minimal free-tier allowance, especially if clients implement automated retries, as each retry constitutes a new request and a new quota reservation [[1]].

Beyond the hard time limits, the architectural design of ZeroGPU is antithetical to the needs of a persistent agent. ZeroGPU is not a persistent GPU host but rather a special Gradio runtime with a lifecycle that is scoped to a single HTTP request [[2]]. Code that requires GPU acceleration must be placed within a function decorated with `@spaces.GPU`. All code outside this decorator runs in PyTorch's CUDA-emulation mode on the CPU [[2]]. This request-scoped lifecycle means that any application state maintained within the agent—for example, conversation history, loaded model parameters, or open API connections—is completely destroyed once the HTTP request that initiated the call is complete. This statelessness prevents the maintenance of long-term sessions or memory, which are core requirements for most sophisticated agent architectures. Every new interaction with the agent would require a fresh initialization, re-loading of models, and re-establishment of connections, leading to unacceptable latency and a broken user experience.

This principle of statelessness is reinforced by the fact that Hugging Face Spaces automatically rebuild from a clean environment whenever they restart [[5]]. This means that any data stored in memory during a previous session is permanently lost, forcing the application to re-initialize from scratch [[5]]. This automatic session reset is a critical flaw for any agent requiring continuity. Furthermore, dependency drift is a common cause of unexpected failures in Spaces, where a Space might rebuild using different library versions than expected, causing startup crashes [[5]]. While pinning dependencies can mitigate this, it highlights the inherent instability of the platform's configuration stack, which includes source files, a YAML block in the README.md, `requirements.txt`, selected hardware, and the runtime image [[5]]. This complexity contributes to a fragile environment that is poorly suited for production-grade, autonomous operation.

Finally, the ZeroGPU environment is susceptible to subtle and difficult-to-debug operational failures that manifest as hangs or timeouts. A common cause of "inference hangs" is not the model computation itself, but the process of returning the result to the user interface [[2]]. Returning large objects, CUDA tensors, or malformed image/audio objects can cause the UI to spin indefinitely until a timeout occurs [[2]]. Asynchronous operations in PyTorch can also create misleading stack traces, where an error from a previous operation is reported at a later point in the code, complicating debugging efforts [[2]]. Deadlocks can occur if different parts of the same Space make internal API calls to each other, creating a circular wait condition where each part is waiting for the other to release a resource or GPU quota [[2]]. These hidden failure modes are catastrophic for a production agent, as they can cause the agent to become unresponsive and require manual intervention to recover. The platform also has documented compatibility limits compared to a standard GPU Space, indicating it may not support all features or behaviors of a full GPU instance [[2]]. In conclusion, Hugging Face's ZeroGPU is explicitly designed for interactive demos and short-lived inference tasks, not for long-running, autonomous agents. The combination of hard daily quotas, a request-scoped lifecycle that prevents state maintenance, and a propensity for hidden operational failures makes it fundamentally incapable of supporting a 24/7 workload.

## Continuous Integration Runners: A Fragile Pathway with Hidden Costs

Continuous Integration (CI) platforms such as GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, and CircleCI are powerful tools designed for automating the build, test, and deployment pipeline of software projects. Their free tiers offer a substantial amount of compute time, which has led some developers to explore them as a potential substrate for hosting long-running processes, including AI agents. However, this approach is fraught with fundamental limitations related to job timeouts, finite monthly quotas, and operational constraints that make it an unreliable and ultimately unsustainable solution for 24/7 agent workloads. While they provide a tempting amount of free compute, their core architecture is geared towards ephemeral, discrete tasks rather than continuous operation.

The most immediate and absolute barrier to using CI runners for a 24/7 agent is the imposition of strict maximum job runtime limits. These limits are enforced by all major CI providers and serve to prevent runaway jobs from consuming excessive resources.
*   **GitHub Actions:** Jobs running on standard, hosted runners are limited to a maximum execution time of 6 hours [[10,11,12]]. If a job exceeds this duration, it is automatically terminated and marked as failed [[11,12]]. This 6-hour limit is the default `timeout-minutes` setting of 360 minutes [[13]]. While it is possible to increase this limit on self-hosted runners, the standard hosted runner limit remains a hard ceiling [[14]].
*   **GitLab CI:** By default, the maximum time for a single job is 60 minutes [[23]]. Although this can be configured per project or runner, it still imposes a firm upper bound on process duration.
*   **CircleCI:** All jobs have a maximum life of 5 hours [[18]].

These timeout limits are incompatible with the definition of a 24/7 workload. An agent attempting to run continuously would inevitably hit one of these walls. This necessitates a fragile workaround involving a periodic self-restart mechanism, which would disrupt any continuous process, break in-memory state, and interrupt any ongoing tasks. Furthermore, many CI platforms enforce additional safety measures. CircleCI, for example, implements a "no output timeout" of 10 minutes by default, which kills any job that has been inactive and produced no new logs for that duration [[19]]. This is a common failure mode for complex agents that may be waiting for an external response or processing a large dataset, and it would cause the agent to fail prematurely.

Compounding the issue of job timeouts are the strict limits on concurrency and total compute minutes imposed on free tiers. These quotas govern how much work can be done over a given period and are easily exhausted by a 24/7 process.
*   **GitHub Actions:** The free plan for private repositories provides 2,000 Linux-equivalent compute minutes per month, plus 500 MB of artifact storage [[10,11]]. Public repositories have unlimited access to the free tier [[10]]. Concurrent jobs are also limited to 20 on the free plan [[10]].
*   **GitLab CI:** The free tier is less generous, offering 400 compute minutes per namespace per month [[26,27]]. Some sources indicate this is 400 minutes per month for a top-level group, covering up to 5 users [[26]].
*   **CircleCI:** The free tier provides 30,000 credits per month, which translates to a variable number of build minutes depending on the executor type used [[15]].

Running an agent 24/7 would consume compute minutes at a rate of 1,440 minutes per month. This figure vastly exceeds the monthly allowances of both GitHub Actions (2,000 minutes) and GitLab CI (400 minutes). For example, a single 24-hour run would consume nearly 75% of the entire monthly free allowance on GitLab CI and over 70% of the allowance on GitHub Actions for private repositories. This makes continuous operation financially unsustainable on a free plan and forces frequent pauses or reliance on public repositories, which may have other security and privacy implications.

The only plausible, albeit imperfect, workaround to overcome the shorter job timeouts is to use a self-hosted runner. On GitHub Actions, a job running on a self-hosted runner has a much longer execution ceiling of 5 days [[12,20]]. This significantly extends the window of opportunity for an agent to run continuously without interruption. However, this path introduces a new and significant financial risk. As of March 1, 2026, GitHub will begin charging for self-hosted runner usage, introducing a per-minute fee [[15,21]]. What was previously a viable, cost-free method of extending runtime becomes a metered service, directly contradicting the premise of using "free" compute tiers. This change creates a planning risk for teams that have relied on self-hosted runners for long-running tasks [[15]]. Additionally, self-hosting requires managing the underlying hardware and software, including handling OS updates, security patches, and dependency drift, which adds a layer of operational overhead that may not be feasible for all users [[5]].

In summary, CI runner environments are fundamentally unsuited for 24/7 agent workloads due to their rigid job timeout policies and finite monthly quotas. The most promising workaround, using a self-hosted runner, is rendered less attractive by the impending introduction of charges and the associated operational burden.

## Comparative Analysis of Limitations and Constraints

To determine the suitability of various free-tier compute offerings for 24/7 agent workloads, a direct comparison of their core limitations and constraints is essential. Each category—serverless GPUs, Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU, and CI runners—presents a unique set of architectural and commercial barriers that conflict with the demands of persistent, uninterrupted operation. The analysis reveals that none of these options are inherently designed for such workloads, but they fail for different reasons and to varying degrees of severity.

The following table provides a structured comparison of the key attributes relevant to hosting a 24/7 agent:

| Attribute | Serverless GPU Platforms | Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU | CI Runner Environments |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Use Case** | Bursty inference, batch processing, asynchronous tasks [[16]] | Interactive demos, short-lived inference [[2]] | Software build, test, and deployment automation [[17]] |
| **Max Job Runtime** | Configurable, often up to 24 hours (e.g., Modal) [[24]] | Not Applicable (Request-scoped) [[2]] | Hosted: 5-6 hours; Self-hosted: 5 days [[10,18,20]] |
| **Daily Quota** | Not Applicable | Yes, Hard Limit: 3.5 min/day (Free Tier) [[3]] | No, Monthly Quota: 400 min/month (GitLab) [[26]] |
| **Monthly Quota** | Yes, Small Credit Grants (e.g., $30/mo on Modal) [[7]] | No, Daily Reset System [[4]] | Yes, Strict Limits (e.g., 2,000 min/mo on GitHub) [[10]] |
| **State Management** | Stateless by default; requires external services [[16]] | Stateless per-request; no persistent memory [[2]] | Stateless between jobs; requires external storage [[5]] |
| **Cold Start Latency** | Low (sub-second to several seconds) [[9,16]] | N/A (Not applicable to persistent hosts) | High (minutes to hours for VM spin-up) |
| **Key Architectural Barrier** | Ephemeral nature, statelessness, and cold starts [[16]] | Hard daily time quotas and request-scoped lifecycle [[2,3]] | Rigid job timeouts and finite monthly quotas [[10,23]] |
| **Commercial Risk** | Volatile market; free tiers are at risk of discontinuation [[29]] | Quotas are strictly enforced and reset daily [[3]] | Quotas are finite and can be exhausted quickly [[10]] |

This comparative view clarifies the distinct failure modes of each platform. Serverless GPU platforms are architecturally misaligned with continuous operation due to their ephemeral, stateless nature and the inherent latency of cold starts. They are optimized for discrete events, not for maintaining a persistent state over time. Hugging Face's ZeroGPU is crippled by a hard, daily time limit that effectively prevents any form of continuous execution beyond a few minutes. Its request-scoped lifecycle means that any attempt to maintain state across interactions is doomed to fail, as the entire environment is reset with each new user request. CI runners are constrained by a combination of rigid job timeouts and consumption caps. While self-hosted runners on GitHub offer a longer runtime window of 5 days, this advantage is undermined by the impending introduction of per-minute charges starting March 1, 2026 [[15,21]].

The table clearly shows that no single option excels in all critical areas. Serverless GPUs offer the longest theoretical runtime but are hampered by statelessness and cold starts. Hugging Face ZeroGPU is the most restrictive with its 3.5-minute daily limit. CI runners provide the most generous monthly quotas (especially for public repos on GitHub), but their 6-hour job timeout on hosted runners is a hard wall for any uninterrupted process. The choice between these options depends on which limitation is considered the most tolerable, but the analysis indicates that all are fundamentally flawed for the stated purpose.

## Operational Viability and Hidden Barriers to Continuous Execution

Beyond the explicit technical specifications like job timeouts and quotas, a comprehensive assessment of free-tier compute for 24/7 agent workloads must consider the myriad of hidden operational barriers that can prevent true continuous execution. These barriers include platform-specific behaviors, environmental instabilities, and terms of service that can lead to abrupt terminations or degraded performance, turning what appears to be a viable setup into a fragile and unreliable system. The research reveals that even if a platform's stated limits were to permit a 24/7 run, numerous hidden factors would likely intervene to disrupt the process.

For Hugging Face Spaces, one of the most significant hidden barriers is the platform's susceptibility to "inference hangs" that appear as indefinite loading screens [[2]]. These hangs are not caused by the model's computation time but by issues in the return path of the prediction. For example, attempting to return a large nested object, a CUDA tensor, or a generator that never terminates can cause the UI to freeze until a timeout occurs [[2]]. This is particularly problematic for an agent that might need to construct complex data structures to respond to a query. Another subtle failure mode is deadlocks that can occur within the same Space. If one part of the application logic makes an internal API call (e.g., via `gradio_client.Client`) to another endpoint within the same Space, and that endpoint is waiting for a resource or GPU quota, a circular wait condition can be created, causing the original request to hang forever [[2]]. Debugging these issues is notoriously difficult, especially since asynchronous CUDA operations in PyTorch can produce misleading stack traces, reporting an error at a subsequent line of code when the actual problem occurred earlier [[2]]. For an autonomous agent, encountering such a deadlock would mean a permanent halt in its operation, requiring manual intervention to restart the Space.

Furthermore, the Hugging Face runtime environment is prone to instability from dependency drift [[5]]. When a Space rebuilds from a clean environment, it pulls dependencies afresh. If a pinned library like Gradio resolves to an older version while an unpinned library like `huggingface_hub` resolves to a newer one (e.g., v1.0+), it can lead to API incompatibilities and startup crashes [[5]]. While pinning conflicting dependencies can resolve this, it highlights the fragility of the configuration stack, which involves multiple components like `requirements.txt`, the README.md YAML block, and the runtime image [[5]]. An agent deployed in this environment could suddenly stop working without any changes to its own code, simply because a dependent library was updated. This lack of environmental consistency is a major operational risk for a system intended to run continuously.

CI runner environments present their own set of hidden operational barriers. One of the most common causes of jobs hitting their timeout limit is a hung process, such as a Docker build step that gets stuck waiting for user input or a test suite that starts a server and never closes it [[13]]. An agent's logic could inadvertently create such a situation, for example, by getting stuck in an infinite loop or failing to close a socket connection. Many platforms also have a "no output" timeout, like CircleCI's default 10-minute limit, which kills any job that becomes unresponsive [[19]]. An agent performing a long, computationally intensive task with infrequent logging output could easily trigger this safeguard and be terminated prematurely. The reliance on external dependencies also introduces risk. An agent might depend on a third-party API or a model hosted elsewhere; if that service becomes unavailable, the agent's execution could stall indefinitely, eventually hitting a timeout.

Self-hosting a CI runner, while extending the maximum job runtime to 5 days on GitHub, introduces a new class of operational burdens [[20]]. The user is now responsible for the health and maintenance of the underlying hardware and operating system. This includes applying security patches, managing disk space, and ensuring the runner software itself is kept up to date. Dependency drift is also a concern here; the agent's environment must be carefully managed to avoid the same issues seen in hosted environments [[5]]. The responsibility for troubleshooting and recovery falls entirely on the user. If the machine loses power, the network connection drops, or the runner process crashes, the agent will stop running, and there is no automated mechanism to restart it within the CI framework itself. While a workflow could be scheduled to periodically check the agent's status and restart it, this adds significant complexity and does not address the root cause of the failure. The impending charge for self-hosted runner usage further complicates the operational picture, transforming a perceived cost-saving measure into a direct, metered expense that must be budgeted for [[21]].

Even serverless GPU platforms, despite their apparent elegance, carry hidden risks. The terms of service often explicitly disclaim any guarantee of uptime or availability, allowing the provider to suspend or terminate a user's account without notice if they are deemed to be "unduly stressing system resources" [[30]]. An agent running continuously, even if highly efficient, could be flagged by the provider's resource monitoring systems and terminated. Providers may also prohibit automated use of their systems, which could encompass the behavior of a background agent, potentially placing the user in violation of the terms of service [[30]]. The business volatility of the serverless GPU market, exemplified by the shutdown of companies like Banana, means that free tiers are particularly at risk of being eliminated or severely curtailed with little warning [[29]]. This uncertainty makes relying on such a platform for any critical, long-term function a risky proposition. In essence, the path to a 24/7 agent workload on any of these free-tier platforms is paved with hidden pitfalls that go far beyond the advertised specifications, demanding a level of operational vigilance and complexity that is often impractical.

## Synthesis and Strategic Recommendations

The comprehensive analysis of free-tier compute offerings from serverless GPU platforms, Hugging Face Spaces, and CI runners converges on a clear and decisive conclusion: none of these options are architecturally or commercially viable for supporting a true 24/7 agent workload. The fundamental design principles of these services prioritize ephemeral, stateless, and metered usage patterns, which are inherently incompatible with the sustained, stateful, and continuous operation required by an autonomous agent. While each category offers compelling value for specific use cases, they are all fundamentally misaligned with the goal of uninterrupted, long-term execution.

Serverless GPU platforms, such as Modal and Runpod, are built around the concept of scale-to-zero, where compute resources spin up on demand and dissipate when idle [[16]]. This model is excellent for batch processing and bursty traffic but fails for persistent agents due to two primary issues: cold starts and statelessness. The latency introduced during a cold start, which can range from sub-second to several seconds, is prohibitive for real-time agents, and the inherent statelessness of the environment makes maintaining a persistent memory or session impossible without complex and costly external state management solutions [[9,16]]. Furthermore, their free tiers consist of small credit grants that are quickly exhausted by any meaningful workload, and their volatile business landscape poses a significant risk to the longevity of these free offerings [[7,29]].

Hugging Face Spaces, particularly with its ZeroGPU feature, is perhaps the most explicitly unsuitable option. Its architecture is deliberately designed for interactive demos and short-lived inference tasks [[2]]. The platform's most critical limitation is its hard daily time quota, which restricts free-tier users to just 3.5 minutes of GPU time per day [[3]]. This single constraint makes any form of continuous operation impossible. Compounding this issue is ZeroGPU's request-scoped lifecycle, where the entire application environment is reset with each new user interaction, erasing any in-memory state and preventing the maintenance of a persistent agent session [[2,5]]. The platform is also rife with hidden operational barriers, such as "inference hangs" and deadlocks, that can cause an agent to become unresponsive and require manual intervention [[2]].

Continuous Integration (CI) runners, offered by providers like GitHub, GitLab, and CircleCI, are the next most plausible option but are ultimately unreliable. Their primary obstacles are strict job timeout limits (typically 5-6 hours on hosted runners) and finite monthly compute quotas [[10,18,23]]. A 24/7 agent would require a fragile self-restart mechanism to survive these cycles, disrupting continuity and breaking any in-memory state. While using a self-hosted runner on GitHub extends the maximum job runtime to 5 days, this advantage is severely undermined by the introduction of a per-minute fee for such usage effective March 1, 2026 [[15,20,21]]. This transforms a free resource into a metered service, reintroducing a direct financial cost and operational overhead associated with maintaining the underlying hardware.

Given these findings, the strategic recommendation is unequivocal: free-tier cloud services should not be considered a foundation for a production-grade, 24/7 agent. Instead, these resources should be reframed and leveraged for their intended purposes within the agent's development and operational lifecycle. CI runners are invaluable for development, testing, and validation, ensuring agent logic is robust before deployment. Serverless GPUs and Hugging Face Jobs are ideal for running periodic, batch-oriented tasks that an agent might trigger, such as model fine-tuning, data processing, or generating periodic reports. Hugging Face Jobs, for instance, offer a configurable timeout of up to 30 minutes, making them suitable for scheduled, long-running but not continuous tasks [[6]].

If the research goal is to find the *least unsuitable* free option, a self-hosted GitHub Actions runner emerges as the weakest link in the chain. It offers the longest potential runtime window of 5 days before needing a restart [[20]]. However, this recommendation comes with severe caveats: it will rapidly exhaust the monthly free minute quotas of other CI systems, introduces a direct operational cost starting in March 2026 [[21]], and requires diligent maintenance of the host machine. Any agent deployed on this platform must have a highly robust self-healing mechanism to survive the inevitable 5-day cycle.

Ultimately, for any serious deployment of a 24/7 agent, a paid service is necessary. The insights gained from this analysis of free tiers are crucial for guiding the selection of a paid plan. Understanding that Modal offers configurable timeouts up to 24 hours [[24]] or that dedicated virtual machines provide guaranteed uptime and control can inform a decision toward a solution better aligned with the agent's operational requirements. The pursuit of a "free" 24/7 agent workload leads down a path of architectural compromise, operational fragility, and hidden costs, confirming that sustainable, continuous operation requires investment in appropriate, paid infrastructure.
