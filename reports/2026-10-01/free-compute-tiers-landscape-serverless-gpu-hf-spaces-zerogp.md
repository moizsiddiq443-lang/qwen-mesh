# From Demo to Deployment: Why Free Compute Tiers Fail to Sustain 24/7 AI Agents

## Architectural Incompatibility of Serverless GPU Platforms

The investigation into the viability of generic serverless GPU platforms for sustaining 24/7 AI agent workloads reveals a profound architectural mismatch between the design philosophy of these services and the core requirements of persistent, stateful applications. These platforms, including prominent offerings from Modal, RunPod, and other similar providers, are fundamentally engineered around a "scale-to-zero" billing model designed for efficiency in handling bursty, event-driven, or intermittent workloads [[14,38]]. This architecture, while highly cost-effective for many AI tasks like batch inference or short-lived interactive demos, is structurally unsuited for the continuous, uninterrupted operation characteristic of an AI agent [[13,29]]. The primary impediment lies in the ephemeral nature of the execution environment; containers are spun up only when a request is received and are automatically terminated after a period of inactivity to conserve resources and prevent users from being billed for idle time [[12,55]].

A critical technical limitation for any agent workload is the default inactivity timeout. For instance, Modal's serverless containers are configured to shut down after just five minutes of no incoming requests [[12]]. This means that unless explicitly managed, an agent's entire runtime context—including loaded model weights, internal memory state, conversation history, and open connections—would be completely erased with every lull in activity [[55]]. Maintaining a persistent state across these inevitable container shutdowns requires deliberate intervention through specific platform features. Modal offers mechanisms such as `container_persistent: true`, which takes a snapshot of the entire sandbox filesystem, and Modal Volumes, which are remote, persistent filesystems that can be mounted to preserve data [[12]]. While powerful, these solutions introduce significant caveats. The persistent sandbox feature, for example, is noted to force non-preemptible pricing, which carries a substantial premium over standard rates and is intended for production-ready applications where guaranteed availability is paramount [[29]]. Similarly, while Modal Volumes provide a solution, they represent a layer of complexity and a potential point of failure or additional cost that is antithetical to the simplicity of a "free" tier. This necessity to pay for state persistence directly contradicts the research goal of finding a zero-cost solution.

Furthermore, the concept of cold starts presents a severe latency challenge for real-time AI agents. When a new request arrives after a container has been terminated, the system must go through a multi-stage initialization process: pulling the container image, loading the model weights into GPU memory, and setting up the CUDA runtime environment [[15]]. This process can take anywhere from 200 milliseconds to over 60 seconds, depending on the size of the model and the optimization of the platform [[15,38]]. In contrast, a warm container that has been kept active can generate tokens in approximately 30 milliseconds, creating a latency gap of over 1000x [[15]]. For an interactive application like a chatbot or an AI agent, waiting 30-60 seconds for a response is unacceptable and would lead to user abandonment [[15]]. Some platforms attempt to mitigate this with features like RunPod's "FlashBoot" or Beam's custom container runtime, which aim to reduce cold start times to sub-second levels [[14,27]]. However, these optimizations are typically geared towards smaller models or are part of paid tiers, and they do not solve the fundamental problem of state loss upon termination. To counteract cold starts, users might be tempted to configure a longer `container_idle_timeout` to keep their agent's container warm [[12]]. While this strategy reduces latency, it also consumes compute credits continuously, effectively turning the pay-per-use model into a subscription-like cost and quickly exhausting the modest free tier allowances [[29]].

The economic structure of these free tiers further solidifies their unsuitability for 24/7 operation. Modal's Starter plan, for example, provides $30 in monthly credits [[29]]. This seemingly generous amount is quickly depleted by even modest workloads; the same source estimates this allowance is sufficient for only about 7-8 hours of continuous H100 GPU time or 27 hours of A10G time per month [[12]]. An AI agent, which may require periodic processing, model updates, or background tasks, would likely exhaust this quota within a single day of operation. The free tier is therefore positioned as a sandbox for experimentation and development, not as a foundation for persistent services [[26]]. This is reinforced by economic analyses showing that for workloads with sustained utilization above approximately 30%, dedicated on-demand instances become more cost-effective than serverless platforms [[38]]. This implies that a truly 24/7 agent, which represents 100% sustained utilization, would almost certainly be more expensive on a serverless provider than on a simple, always-on virtual machine, even if one could find a way to run it for free. The business model is inherently inverted for this use case.

Finally, the hardware and resource allocation in these free tiers is often limited and subject to change. While some platforms may offer access to powerful GPUs, the free plans themselves typically come with lower-tier resource allocations. For example, GitHub's free hosted runners for private repositories are provisioned with fewer CPUs and less RAM compared to those for public repositories [[33]]. Serverless platforms may have separate queues or lower priority for free-tier users, leading to increased wait times for resources [[24]]. The very nature of a shared, pay-per-use pool means that performance is not guaranteed and can fluctuate based on overall platform demand [[52]]. An AI agent cannot afford such unpredictability. It requires a stable and predictable computational environment to function reliably. The combination of ephemeral environments, prohibitive state-persistence costs, unacceptable cold-start latencies, minuscule free-tier allowances, and unpredictable resource allocation makes generic serverless GPU platforms fundamentally incompatible with the goal of sustaining a 24/7 AI agent workload within a zero-cost budget.

## Hugging Face ZeroGPU: A Deep Dive into Quota-Limited Demos

Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU represents a distinct category of free compute, specifically tailored for hosting interactive AI demos and short inference calls rather than for sustained, production-grade workloads [[8,51]]. An analysis of its architecture and policies reveals that it is fundamentally misaligned with the requirements of a 24/7 AI agent. The platform's very name, "ZeroGPU," is potentially misleading; it signifies zero-guarantee and zero-reserved capacity, not zero-cost or zero-unreliability. The service operates on a dynamic, on-demand basis, allocating shared NVIDIA GPUs from a communal pool as needed [[2]]. This model is optimized for sporadic user interaction, where a GPU is requested, used for a brief period, and then released back to the pool for others to use [[2]]. This stands in stark contrast to an AI agent, which requires a predictable, available, and persistent computational resource to maintain its state and perform continuous operations without interruption.

The primary barrier to using ZeroGPU for a 24/7 agent is its multi-layered quota system, which is explicitly designed to limit total daily usage. Free personal accounts are granted a daily GPU-minute quota, though the exact number appears to vary slightly across sources and may depend on authentication status, with figures cited as 2 minutes for unauthenticated users, 3.5 minutes for authenticated users, and 5 minutes for free accounts [[3,4,5]]. More critically, this time-based limit is compounded by a separate, undocumented request-based limit. According to community reports, free users are restricted to a mere three runs or requests per day on their ZeroGPU Space [[4,5]]. This dual constraint makes continuous operation nearly impossible. An agent attempting to poll for tasks or run a background loop would likely hit the request limit within hours, regardless of whether its cumulative GPU time has been exhausted. The system is engineered to be consumed in short bursts, not sustained engagement.

Furthermore, the allocation mechanism itself is unreliable and lacks guarantees. Users frequently report encountering errors such as "No GPU was available after 60s," indicating that the system's queue can be congested [[24]]. This issue persists even for paid PRO subscribers, who are expected to receive higher priority, suggesting that availability is a matter of first-come, first-served pool exhaustion rather than a privilege tied to subscription level [[24]]. The error message presented to a logged-in, paid user—"No GPU was available after 60s [Create a free account] to get a higher priority in ZeroGPU queues"—is described as highly confusing, as it incorrectly frames the request as originating from an unauthenticated guest, ignoring the user's actual subscription status [[24]]. This lack of reliability is a fatal flaw for any application requiring uninterrupted service. An AI agent cannot afford to fail simply because another user is currently utilizing the shared GPU. The platform's Terms of Service explicitly reserve the right to modify, suspend, or discontinue any service at any time, with or without notice, adding another layer of uncertainty [[6]].

The technical architecture of ZeroGPU reinforces its position as a demo hosting platform. It exclusively supports the Gradio SDK (version 4+), PyTorch, and specific Python versions, imposing strict compatibility requirements [[2]]. Crucially, to be eligible for the ZeroGPU tier, applications must use the `@spaces.GPU` decorator, a feature that was not present in older templates, leading to a dramatic increase in failure rates for copied spaces after the policy change [[1]]. This decorator signals to the platform that a function is GPU-intensive and should be executed on the shared infrastructure. However, this execution is strictly transient; a real GPU is only allocated when the decorated function is called and is immediately released upon completion [[2]]. There is no persistent session or state maintained between calls, a feature that is absolutely essential for an AI agent that needs to remember past interactions, manage its goals, and maintain its operational context over time. The platform's focus is on serving individual predictions, not on running a continuous process.

Hugging Face's own documentation and evolving policies signal a clear intent to manage ZeroGPU as a limited resource. The company has moved to restrict access to the free GPU tier, requiring accounts to have a verified email and be older than 30 days to qualify [[1]]. New accounts created after this policy shift have no access to any free GPU option whatsoever [[1]]. Additionally, Hugging Face has made it clear that its CPU Basic Spaces, which are intended for prototypes and demos, are explicitly capped at 2 vCPUs and 16 GB of RAM, making them inadequate for most generative models and positioning them as unsuitable for sustained production workloads [[8]]. This strategic direction suggests that the platform is actively managing its free offerings to serve specific use cases (interactive demos) and is discouraging their use for general-purpose, persistent computing. Any reliance on ZeroGPU for a 24/7 agent is therefore built on a foundation of a service that is intentionally scarce, unreliable, architecturally inappropriate, and subject to arbitrary policy changes.

## CI Runner Environments: The Unsuitability of Ephemeral Build Systems

Continuous Integration/Continuous Delivery (CI/CD) runner environments provided by platforms like GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, and CircleCI are perhaps the least suitable category of free compute for sustaining a 24/7 AI agent workload. These systems are meticulously architected for a fundamentally different purpose: executing discrete, finite, and idempotent jobs related to building, testing, and deploying software code [[20,33]]. Their core design principle is ephemerality; each job runs in a fresh, disposable, and isolated environment, with no expectation of state persistence from one execution to the next [[20,37]]. This inherent architecture makes them fundamentally incompatible with the continuous, stateful nature of an AI agent, which requires a long-running process that maintains its context and memory over extended periods.

The most immediate and insurmountable barrier is the imposition of hard runtime limits on all major platforms. GitHub Actions, for instance, enforces a maximum job execution time of 6 hours on its hosted runners, a limit that applies universally and cannot be easily overridden, even on self-hosted runners [[16,17]]. Similarly, GitLab CI imposes a 3-hour maximum runtime for jobs on its shared runners [[20]], and CircleCI's free plan has a hard limit of 1 hour per job [[59,60]]. A 24/7 AI agent would constantly exceed these limits, causing its process to be forcibly terminated and restarted every few hours. This repeated cycle of termination and restart would prevent the agent from ever achieving a stable, continuous state. While self-hosted runners on GitHub can run for up to 5 days, this is a configurable limit and still subject to the platform's cancellation policies if a job remains queued for too long [[16,30]]. The existence of these strict time limits confirms that the platforms are not designed for long-running processes.

Beyond runtime limits, the ephemeral nature of the execution environment poses a second critical obstacle. Each CI job executes in its own newly provisioned virtual machine or container, which is deleted immediately after the job completes [[20,33]]. This architecture provides excellent security and isolation but makes state persistence impossible without complex and fragile workarounds. An agent's state—such as its current task list, memory buffers, or loaded model parameters—cannot be stored in the local filesystem, as it will be wiped clean after each job runs. While caching mechanisms exist, they are intended for preserving dependencies like package managers or model artifacts between jobs, not for storing the dynamic state of an application [[20]]. Mounting external storage like Google Drive, as is possible in environments like Google Colab, is not a native or reliable feature of standard CI runners and introduces its own set of complexities and limitations, such as temporary disconnections [[52,54]]. The system is designed for "fire-and-forget" tasks, not for hosting a persistent service.

Perhaps the most significant and legally binding barrier is found within the Acceptable Use Policies (AUPs) of these platforms. GitHub's AUP explicitly prohibits "automated excessive bulk activity and coordinated inauthentic activity" and forbids using their servers for any form of "time-sharing or service bureau purposes" [[49]]. Using a CI runner to host a continuously running agent that accepts external requests would almost certainly fall under this definition of prohibited activity. The policy gives GitHub the right to suspend accounts that place an undue burden on their infrastructure [[49]]. GitLab has similarly tightened its policies, introducing new rate limits specifically to manage traffic from AI agents and automated tools. Starting October 19, 2026, unauthenticated API requests from any IP address will be capped at 60 requests per hour, a measure designed to curb abuse from automated processes [[40,41]]. Even for authenticated users, rate limits are tiered and relatively low for free accounts (e.g., 60 requests/hour for unauthenticated, 5,000/hour for Free-tier users), making it impractical for an agent that requires frequent polling or interaction [[40,41]]. These policies are direct legal prohibitions against the kind of sustained, automated workload an agent entails.

Even the practice of self-hosting runners, a workaround for quota limits, fails to provide a viable path to a free 24/7 solution. While self-hosting allows users to bypass the minute limits imposed by platforms like GitLab [[19,37]], it introduces substantial operational overhead and, critically, new financial costs [[22]]. Managing the underlying infrastructure, keeping the runners updated, and ensuring high availability requires significant effort [[48]]. More importantly, GitHub recently introduced a new $0.002 per minute platform fee for orchestrating jobs on self-hosted runners in private repositories [[22,48]]. This transforms the "free" act of self-hosting into a recurring monthly expense—for a single runner running 24/7, this fee amounts to approximately $86.40 per month [[48]]. This new cost structure completely undermines the premise of a zero-cost deployment. Therefore, CI runner environments, whether hosted or self-hosted, are structurally, contractually, and financially unsuited for the goal of sustaining a 24/7 AI agent workload.

## Contractual Barriers and Anti-Abuse Policy Enforcement

Beyond the technical and architectural limitations, a critical dimension of this analysis involves examining the contractual obligations and acceptable use policies (AUPs) that govern the free compute tiers. These terms, often buried in lengthy documents, serve as the ultimate enforcement mechanism, granting platforms the legal right to deny service, suspend accounts, or terminate access for activities deemed abusive or outside the intended scope of the free offering. For a 24/7 AI agent workload, which by its nature involves sustained, automated, and resource-intensive operation, these contractual barriers present a formidable and often overlooked hurdle. Relying on a free tier for such a purpose is not merely a technical gamble but also a significant legal and financial risk.

The language in the AUPs of these platforms is often deliberately broad, allowing companies to interpret and enforce policies against emerging or unforeseen use cases. For example, CircleCI's AUP explicitly prohibits using their services "for the benefit of any third party" and forbids "any form of excessive automated bulk activity such as spamming...or mining or demonstrating proof-of-work or other proof by use of resources for any cryptocurrency or blockchain" [[57]]. While "mining" is mentioned, the prohibition on "excessive automated bulk activity" and providing services to third parties is far more expansive. Hosting a backend for an AI agent that other applications interact with could be construed as providing a "service" to a third party, thus violating this clause. Similarly, GitHub's AUP contains a strong prohibition against using the service as a "time-sharing or service bureau," a term that directly describes the act of renting out computational resources to others [[49]]. The policy also broadly forbids any activity that constitutes "excessive automated bulk activity" and reserves the right to suspend accounts that place an "undue burden" on their infrastructure [[49]]. Given that a 24/7 agent would be a continuous consumer of resources, it is highly probable that its usage pattern would be flagged as excessive by the platform's automated monitoring systems.

GitLab has also taken proactive steps to address the rise of AI-driven automation on its platform. The company has announced plans to implement stricter rate limits specifically targeting automated tools, reducing the cap on unauthenticated requests from 500 per minute to just 60 per hour [[40,41]]. This policy change was explicitly motivated by the growing demand from AI agents and automated development tools, signaling that platforms are becoming increasingly vigilant about this type of sustained activity [[41]]. The new limits apply per user identity, meaning that minting multiple personal access tokens for the same user will not circumvent the quota; a separate user or service account would be required, which still falls under the plan's subscription tier limits [[40]]. This demonstrates a clear corporate strategy to manage infrastructure load from persistent, automated workloads, making it a risky endeavor to operate a 24/7 agent on GitLab.com's free tier.

Even on platforms that do not have explicitly worded anti-abuse clauses, the potential for conflict arises from the fundamental misunderstanding of the service's purpose. Hugging Face, for instance, has implemented policies that restrict access to its ZeroGPU tier and explicitly positions its CPU Basic Spaces as being intended for prototypes and interactive demos, not for sustained production workloads [[8]]. The Content Policy prohibits "platform abuse," which includes using unauthorized remote management tools to bypass restrictions [[9]]. While not directly referencing AI agents, the principle of using the platform for a purpose other than its intended design could be grounds for action. Furthermore, Hugging Face's general Terms of Service grant the company the right to modify, suspend, or discontinue any service at any time, with or without notice, and they explicitly disclaim all warranties, stating the service is provided "as is" [[6]]. This contractual flexibility means that a platform could alter its free tier policies or outright remove a feature, leaving a dependent agent without its computational foundation.

The practical enforcement of these policies can be swift and punitive. Users of Hugging Face have reported sudden blocking of their API keys with 402 errors, citing exceeded quotas, even when they believed they were operating within limits [[7]]. This lack of transparency regarding policy changes and quota adjustments creates a precarious environment where a developer's project can be abruptly disrupted [[7]]. On GitHub, users have had their Actions capabilities disabled at the account level due to heavy CI/CD usage, even for legitimate open-source projects, suggesting that the line between acceptable and excessive use is enforced automatically and can be difficult to navigate [[30]]. For a 24/7 AI agent, which by its very nature will exhibit consistent, high-volume usage patterns, the risk of triggering these automated abuse detection systems is extremely high. The combination of broad, ambiguous language in AUPs, proactive rate limiting, and the unilateral right to modify or terminate services makes relying on any of these free tiers for a critical, long-running application an inherently unstable and legally uncertain proposition.

## Workarounds and Hidden Costs of Bypassing Platform Limits

In the pursuit of a sustainable 24/7 AI agent workload on a zero-cost budget, users inevitably explore various workarounds to circumvent the stringent limitations imposed by free compute tiers. These strategies often appear promising on the surface but invariably introduce hidden costs, either in the form of financial expenditure, immense operational overhead, or heightened contractual risk. A thorough analysis reveals that there is no truly "free" path to achieving persistent, uninterrupted operation, as any successful workaround ultimately shifts the burden from one domain to another, undermining the core objective of the research.

One of the most commonly discussed workarounds is the creation of multiple accounts to aggregate free tier allowances. For example, a user might create several Hugging Face accounts, each qualifying for a PRO subscription, thereby doubling or tripling their daily ZeroGPU quota [[18]]. While this technique can temporarily increase available resources, it is explicitly considered a violation of Hugging Face's Terms of Service [[18]]. Such practices are often referred to as "account sharing" or "multi-accounting." Engaging in this behavior carries a significant risk of account termination for all involved accounts, rendering the entire strategy unsustainable and dangerous [[18]]. This approach trades the initial platform-imposed limit for a much greater risk of losing access entirely, making it an untenable solution for any serious project.

Another prevalent workaround involves shifting from hosted runners to self-hosted runners, particularly on platforms like GitLab and GitHub. Self-hosted runners allow users to connect their own infrastructure (a personal server, a VPS, or a local machine) to the CI/CD platform, effectively bypassing the platform's minute quotas and resource limits [[19,37]]. On GitLab, this is a valid strategy for avoiding the 400-minute monthly limit on shared runners [[19]]. However, this solution comes with substantial trade-offs. First, it transfers the cost of the free tier from platform minutes to the cost of the underlying hardware and internet connectivity. Second, and more critically, it introduces massive operational overhead. Users are now responsible for provisioning, securing, updating, and maintaining the runner infrastructure, as well as managing the runner software itself [[22,48]]. Debugging issues, ensuring scalability, and dealing with hardware failures all fall on the user, transforming a simple deployment process into a full-fledged DevOps responsibility [[22]].

This operational burden is compounded by new financial costs that were previously avoided. As of March 1, 2026, GitHub began charging a $0.002 per minute platform fee for orchestrating jobs on self-hosted runners in private repositories [[22,48]]. This fee covers the scheduling and workflow automation services that GitHub provides, effectively monetizing the control plane that was once free [[47]]. For a self-hosted runner running 24/7, this translates to a recurring monthly cost of approximately $86.40, completely negating the "free" aspect of the solution [[48]]. This policy change by GitHub demonstrates a clear trend: platforms are moving to charge for the orchestration and management layer, regardless of where the actual computation occurs. Thus, what seemed like a clever way to bypass quotas has become a more expensive and complex alternative to using a paid, fully managed service.

For platforms that do not offer self-hosting options, other creative but fragile workarounds emerge. On GitHub, scheduled workflows that use the `schedule` event are effectively disabled for private repositories on the free tier, a poorly documented limitation [[31]]. A workaround involves using an external cron service (like cron-job.org) to periodically trigger the workflow's `workflow_dispatch` API endpoint [[31]]. This introduces a dependency on an external service and requires managing secrets and API tokens securely. It is a brittle solution prone to failure and adds complexity to the deployment pipeline. Similarly, on Hugging Face, users have attempted to work around the restrictive daily quotas by using techniques like predictive scaling or aggressive caching, but these methods are complex to implement and do not solve the fundamental problem of quota exhaustion [[15]]. The need for such intricate and fragile workarounds underscores the fact that the platforms are not designed for this use case, and any deviation from the intended workflow is fraught with difficulty.

Ultimately, all workarounds highlight a central truth: the platforms' free tiers are not intended for persistent workloads. They are designed as a gateway to paid services, with limitations that are carefully calibrated to encourage upgrades. Whether the cost is financial (the new GitHub fee), operational (managing self-hosted runners), or contractual (violating ToS with multiple accounts), the hidden costs of bypassing these limits are substantial. The investigation concludes that the effort and risk required to maintain a 24/7 agent using these workarounds far outweigh the benefits, and no viable, truly free solution exists within the documented boundaries of these platforms.

## Synthesis and Final Assessment of Viability

The comprehensive analysis of free compute tiers from generic serverless GPU providers, Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU, and CI runner environments yields a definitive and unequivocal conclusion: none of these platforms offer a viable, sustainable, or contractually sound method for supporting a 24/7 AI agent workload within their stated free tiers. The investigation reveals a fundamental and irreconcilable conflict between the design principles of these free services and the operational requirements of a persistent, stateful AI agent. The platforms are architected around ephemeral, event-driven, or bursty workloads where idle time is not billed, whereas an AI agent necessitates a continuous, uninterrupted, and predictable computational environment [[12,13,20]].

The core of the issue lies in a confluence of architectural, technical, and contractual barriers. Architecturally, serverless GPU platforms like Modal and RunPod are built on a "scale-to-zero" model that terminates containers after short periods of inactivity, erasing an agent's vital state [[12,55]]. CI runners from GitHub, GitLab, and CircleCI are designed for discrete, finite jobs with strict maximum runtimes of 1 to 6 hours, making continuous operation technically impossible [[16,20,59]]. Hugging Face ZeroGPU is explicitly a demo-hosting service with no guarantee of resource availability, governed by multi-layered quotas that would be exhausted within hours [[4,5,8]].

These architectural flaws are reinforced by concrete technical limitations. All platforms impose hard quotas—whether in daily GPU minutes, monthly build minutes, or request counts—that are insufficient for sustained operation [[5,30,56]]. The need to maintain agent state across these ephemeral environments forces users to rely on paid features like persistent volumes or sandboxes, directly contradicting the zero-cost premise [[12,29]]. Furthermore, the high latency associated with cold starts on scale-to-zero platforms renders them unsuitable for real-time agent interaction [[15,38]]. The economic models are also inverted for this use case; the free allowances are trivially small, and for sustained workloads, dedicated instances are demonstrably cheaper than serverless alternatives [[12,38]].

Contractually, the situation is equally prohibitive. The Acceptable Use Policies of these platforms explicitly forbid the kind of "excessive automated bulk activity" and "time-sharing" that a 24/7 agent constitutes [[49,57]]. Platforms are actively implementing measures to throttle or limit the use of their services by AI agents, viewing sustained, automated workloads as a potential strain on their infrastructure [[40,41]]. The unilateral right reserved by these companies to modify or suspend services without notice, coupled with the prohibition of abusive use, places any project relying on a free tier at extreme risk of sudden and complete failure [[6,9]].

Any potential workarounds, such as creating multiple accounts or self-hosting runners, prove to be double-edged swords. They may bypass certain technical limits but introduce significant new costs, either in the form of financial fees (e.g., GitHub's $0.002/min charge for self-hosted runners [[48]]) or immense operational overhead, ultimately failing to deliver a truly free solution [[18,22]]. The exploration of these paths confirms that the platforms' free tiers are not designed for the user's goal but rather function as a controlled gateway to paid services.

In summary, the landscape of free compute is not conducive to the deployment of 24/7 AI agents. The architectural foundations are misaligned, technical quotas are insufficient, and contractual terms are restrictive. While these free tiers remain invaluable for prototyping, experimentation, and small-scale batch processing, they are fundamentally unsuitable for the demands of a persistent, stateful, and continuously running application. The final assessment is that achieving the research goal is not feasible within the confines of the provided free compute ecosystems.
