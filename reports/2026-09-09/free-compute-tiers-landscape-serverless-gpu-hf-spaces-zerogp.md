# Why Free Compute Tiers Fail at 24/7 AI Agent Workloads: A Deep Dive into Architectural and Policy Barriers

The proliferation of cloud-based infrastructure has democratized access to powerful computational resources, enabling developers and researchers to experiment with complex artificial intelligence models. Among the most sought-after resources are Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), which are essential for training and running many modern AI applications. In response to this demand, major technology providers have introduced "free tier" offerings, designed to lower the barrier to entry for new users. These tiers typically provide limited amounts of compute, storage, and network resources at no cost. Concurrently, the emergence of autonomous AI agents—systems capable of performing complex tasks through reasoning, planning, and tool use—has created a new class of application with distinct infrastructure requirements. A critical requirement for many of these agents is the need for continuous, uninterrupted operation, often described as a 24/7 workload. This report conducts a deep comparative analysis of three prominent categories of free compute tiers—serverless GPU platforms, Hugging Face Spaces with ZeroGPU capabilities, and Continuous Integration (CI) runner environments—to evaluate their technical feasibility and policy compliance for supporting such persistent agent workloads. The analysis will scrutinize each option's underlying architecture, resource quotas, uptime guarantees, and terms of service to determine if they can legally and practically sustain an always-on agent.

## Serverless GPU Platforms: The Unsuitability of On-Demand Compute

Serverless GPU platforms represent a significant evolution in cloud computing, specifically tailored for the demands of modern AI workloads [[17,19]]. Providers such as Runpod, Modal, Google Cloud Run, and Azure Container Apps offer developers the ability to execute GPU-accelerated code without the burden of provisioning, managing, or paying for idle hardware [[16,83]]. The core architectural principle of these services is "scale-to-zero," where GPU instances are spun up only when a request arrives, perform the necessary computation, and are then automatically shut down during periods of inactivity [[75]]. This model stands in stark contrast to traditional virtual machines or dedicated GPU servers, which require payment for the entire duration they are running, regardless of utilization [[49]]. For tasks like AI inference, where requests can be sporadic, this pay-as-you-go approach offers substantial cost savings and operational efficiency [[54]]. Leading providers emphasize features that cater to this bursty workload pattern, such as per-second billing and extremely low latency cold starts, with some boasting sub-200ms startup times for their GPU workers [[90]]. However, this very architecture, while efficient for its intended use case, renders these platforms fundamentally unsuitable for hosting a 24/7 agent workload.

The free offerings from serverless GPU providers are consistently structured as introductory programs rather than sustainable, long-term solutions for continuous operation. These free tiers typically manifest in one of two forms: a fixed amount of subscription credits provided upon signup or a small, quota-limited allocation of compute time [[20]]. For instance, the documentation for Serverless AI explicitly states that its "Free Tier" includes a certain amount of Subscription Credits, which are detailed on the pricing page [[20]]. Similarly, other sources describe free tiers as including "generous compute credits" but quickly contextualize them within the high cost of production-grade hardware [[22]]. One source highlights that a single A100 workload can cost around $246 per month, implicitly illustrating the vast chasm between the value of free credits and the sustained consumption required for an always-on agent [[22]]. While some platforms may offer more generous initial credits, they are universally understood to be temporary incentives to attract new users to their paid ecosystems. There is no evidence in the provided materials to suggest that any serverless GPU provider offers a free tier that can sustain a continuous workload indefinitely. The business model itself is predicated on serving many short-lived, high-throughput jobs; an agent running 24/7 would consume resources continuously without generating the bursty traffic patterns these systems are optimized for, making it economically unviable for the provider to support such a use case for free.

Beyond the quantitative limitations of free credits and quotas, the architectural design of serverless platforms presents an insurmountable obstacle to persistent workloads. The "scale-to-zero" mechanism is the primary culprit. When a serverless GPU instance is idle, it is terminated to conserve resources and costs. An AI agent, by definition, requires a persistent environment to maintain its state, memory, and connections over extended periods [[23]]. If the underlying GPU is shut down, the agent's state is lost, and it must restart from scratch, leading to immediate failure. Even if a provider offered a theoretical "always-on" serverless option, the economic model remains incompatible. These platforms are built to serve thousands of intermittent jobs efficiently; dedicating a GPU to a single agent that never goes idle would be highly inefficient from a resource utilization standpoint and directly contradicts the core value proposition of the service. The very abstraction that makes serverless computing appealing—the separation of code from infrastructure—is antithetical to the needs of an agent that requires a stable, persistent host environment. Furthermore, the concept of a "cold start" is irrelevant for a 24/7 agent, yet it is a central metric used by providers to market their performance, underscoring the mismatch in workload expectations [[17,32]].

The table below outlines key characteristics of serverless GPU platforms and highlights the inherent conflicts with a 24/7 agent workload.

| Feature | Serverless GPU Platform Design | Implication for a 24/7 Agent |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Use Case** | On-demand AI inference, batch processing, and other bursty workloads [[54,75]]. | An agent requires continuous, stateful operation, which is the opposite of a bursty workload. |
| **Scaling Model** | Scale-to-zero: Instances spin up on demand and terminate when idle [[75]]. | A 24/7 agent cannot tolerate being terminated and must maintain a persistent host environment. |
| **Billing Model** | Per-second or per-millisecond billing based on active compute time [[90]]. | While attractive for cost-efficiency, it does not address the problem of resource availability for a non-bursty workload. |
| **Idle Timeout** | Short idle timeouts are common to trigger scale-to-zero behavior [[99,100]]. | Any period of inactivity, even brief, would cause the agent's environment to be destroyed. |
| **State Management** | Stateless by design; the platform abstracts away the underlying server state. | Agents rely on maintaining state across actions and interactions, which is impossible in a stateless environment. |

In summary, serverless GPU platforms, despite their power and flexibility for on-demand AI tasks, are fundamentally misaligned with the requirements of a 24/7 agent workload. Their business model, architectural design, and free-tier offerings are all geared towards ephemeral, intermittent use. Attempting to force a persistent agent onto this model would lead to constant termination, state loss, and likely violate the provider's terms of service, resulting in service disruption and potential account penalties.

## Hugging Face ZeroGPU: Designed for Demos, Not Persistent Agents

Hugging Face has become a central hub for the machine learning community, providing tools and infrastructure to build, train, and deploy AI models. A cornerstone of its ecosystem is "Spaces," a platform for creating and sharing interactive web applications, often demos showcasing AI models [[33]]. To make GPU-powered demos accessible to everyone, Hugging Face developed ZeroGPU, a dynamic and shared GPU allocation system [[11]]. The architecture of ZeroGPU is explicitly designed for its intended purpose: powering demo applications that experience periods of inactivity followed by sudden spikes of user interaction. Instead of reserving a dedicated GPU for a Space 24/7, ZeroGPU allows a Space to "briefly grab" a GPU from a shared pool only when it receives a request [[33]]. This model optimizes resource utilization and allows users to run powerful AI applications without the cost of a permanently allocated GPU [[45]]. However, this very design makes ZeroGPU profoundly unsuitable for hosting a persistent, 24/7 agent workload, whose core requirement is continuous availability and readiness to act.

The most significant and immediate barrier to using ZeroGPU for a continuous agent is the strict enforcement of daily time quotas. These quotas limit the amount of GPU time a user can consume each day, and they reset on a 24-hour cycle [[81]]. The quota varies based on the user's subscription level. According to the documentation, free accounts receive a daily quota of 3.5 minutes, while PRO users get a larger, but still limited, quota of 25 minutes [[44,86]]. Even for a PRO subscriber, 25 minutes is far less than the 1,440 minutes required for a full day of continuous operation. Once a user exhausts their daily quota, their Space becomes unavailable for any GPU-accelerated tasks until the next reset period begins [[84]]. An agent attempting to run continuously would be perpetually stuck waiting for its quota to replenish, rendering it non-functional for most of the day. This quota system is a deliberate architectural choice to prevent abuse and ensure fair resource distribution among the large number of demo creators on the platform. It fundamentally precludes any form of sustained, uninterrupted operation.

Beyond the hard limit of the daily quota, the underlying mechanics of the ZeroGPU allocation system present further obstacles. When a Space requests a GPU, it enters a queue. If no GPU is immediately available in the shared pool, the request waits until one becomes free [[35]]. For an agent trying to maintain a constant connection or listen for events, this queuing process introduces unpredictable delays. More critically, the priority in this queue is directly influenced by the user's remaining GPU quota [[84]]. An agent that has already used its 3.5 or 25 minutes of GPU time for the day would have effectively zero priority, meaning it would wait indefinitely for a GPU to become available. This creates a scenario where the agent is perpetually stuck at "Waiting for a GPU to become available," unable to perform any meaningful work [[35]]. The system is not designed to guarantee access to a GPU; it is designed to allocate scarce resources on a best-effort basis to many competing requests. A 24/7 agent, which represents a single, constant request for a resource, is therefore poorly positioned to succeed in this environment.

The following table details the key aspects of the Hugging Face ZeroGPU system and contrasts them with the requirements of a 24/7 agent.

| Aspect | Hugging Face ZeroGPU System | Requirement for a 24/7 Agent |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Architecture** | Dynamic, shared infrastructure allocating GPUs on-demand for short bursts of activity [[11,33]]. | Requires a persistent, dedicated (or guaranteed) environment to maintain state and avoid termination. |
| **Daily Quota** | Strictly enforced time limit: ~3.5 minutes/day for free users, 25 minutes/day for PRO users [[44,80,86]]. | Requires unlimited, uninterrupted compute time to function continuously. |
| **Availability** | Not guaranteed; requests enter a queue and wait for a free GPU in the shared pool [[35]]. | Requires immediate and guaranteed access to a GPU to respond to events without delay. |
| **Resource Priority** | Priority in the GPU queue is tied to remaining daily quota [[84]]. | Cannot afford to be deprioritized or starved of resources due to quota exhaustion. |
| **Underlying Hardware** | Uses shared NVIDIA GPUs (e.g., A100, H200) with limited vRAM per workload [[12,85]]. | May require consistent, predictable performance and sufficient memory that is not subject to contention. |

Furthermore, there are broader implications related to security and policy compliance. The recent disclosure of a sophisticated intrusion into Hugging Face's production infrastructure, which was driven end-to-end by an autonomous AI agent, underscores the platform's role as a target for agentic activity [[8,10]]. While this incident demonstrated the power of AI agents, it also highlighted the security measures platforms like Hugging Face implement to detect and mitigate anomalous behavior [[46]]. An agent operating outside its intended scope—for example, one that attempts to bypass quotas or runs continuously—could easily be flagged by these security systems as a potential threat. Additionally, all activities on Hugging Face, including those performed by AI agents, are governed by its Content Policy, which is incorporated into its binding Terms of Service [[9]]. Any output generated or action taken by an agent must comply with these rules, adding another layer of legal and operational constraint that could complicate the agent's autonomy [[76]]. Therefore, Hugging Face's ZeroGPU is not just technically incapable of supporting a 24/7 agent due to its quota and allocation system, but it is also a platform that actively monitors for the kind of persistent, stateful behavior that defines such a workload, posing a significant risk of detection and blocking.

## CI Runner Environments: Built for Finite Jobs, Not Stateful Services

Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment (CI/CD) runner environments, exemplified by GitHub Actions and GitLab Runners, are foundational tools in modern software development workflows. Their primary purpose is to automate the processes of building, testing, and deploying code in response to specific events, such as a developer pushing a new commit to a repository or opening a pull request [[68]]. These systems are architected around the concept of a "job"—a discrete, finite sequence of steps that executes and then completes. This fundamental design philosophy makes them inherently unsuitable for hosting a 24/7 agent, which requires a persistent, stateful environment that is always running and ready to accept tasks. The most direct and insurmountable barrier to using these platforms for continuous operation is the imposition of hard runtime limits on every job.

Both GitHub Actions and GitLab Runners enforce strict maximum execution times for jobs running on their hosted infrastructure. For GitHub Actions, any single job is limited to a maximum runtime of six hours [[13,68]]. For GitLab Runners, the limit is even shorter, at three hours [[69]]. These limits apply regardless of the complexity of the job or the amount of work being done. A 24/7 agent, by its very nature, would repeatedly exceed these time limits, causing the job to be forcefully terminated by the platform. While an agent could be programmed to restart itself after a termination, this approach introduces significant reliability issues, downtime, and complexity. The platform's control over the job lifecycle means the agent would constantly be fighting against the system's design constraints, making it an unreliable foundation for any mission-critical task.

While self-hosted runners offer more flexibility by allowing users to provide their own infrastructure, they introduce a new set of prohibitive challenges and risks that render them equally unsuitable for this use case. Self-hosted runners can be configured with much longer timeout durations, with default settings allowing for sessions up to 24 hours [[71]]. However, relying on a self-hosted runner for a 24/7 agent is fraught with difficulties. First, there is the issue of maintenance and management. Users are responsible for ensuring their runner's operating system and software are kept up-to-date. GitHub, for example, specifies that if a software update is not performed within 30 days, the service will stop queuing jobs for that runner, effectively rendering it useless [[14]]. This adds a significant ongoing operational burden. Second, and more critically, there is the risk of automatic suspension. Platforms take remote access to their runner environments very seriously. GitHub, in particular, has been known to trigger automatic security suspensions for even brief or curiosity-driven remote access attempts [[1]]. An agent running continuously on a self-hosted runner might generate network traffic or exhibit behaviors (such as constant polling or outbound connections) that are flagged by automated security detectors as malicious or indicative of a compromised machine, leading to a permanent ban of the associated account [[3,5]]. Finally, there is the issue of inactivity. GitHub will automatically delete self-hosted runners from the system if they have not been used for at least 14 days [[72]]. This means an agent relying on a self-hosted runner would inevitably fail after a two-week period unless it implemented a complex workaround to periodically ping the service to prove its continued existence, defeating the purpose of a simple, persistent process.

The table below compares the characteristics of CI runner environments with the needs of a 24/7 agent.

| Feature | CI Runner Environment (Hosted & Self-Hosted) | Requirement for a 24/7 Agent |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core Purpose** | Automate finite, event-driven software development workflows (build, test, deploy). | Provide a persistent, stateful environment for continuous operation. |
| **Runtime Limits** | Hosted runners have strict limits: 6 hours for GitHub Actions [[13]], 3 hours for GitLab [[69]]. | Requires unlimited, uninterrupted runtime without forced termination. |
| **Self-Hosted Runner Rules** | Subject to automatic deletion after 14 days of inactivity [[72]]; requires manual OS/software updates [[14]]. | Requires a managed, always-available host with minimal maintenance overhead. |
| **Security Risk** | High risk of automatic suspension for remote access or unusual network activity [[1,5]]. | Requires a secure and stable network environment that does not trigger abuse detectors. |
| **State Management** | Jobs are typically stateless; each execution starts fresh. | Requires a stable environment to maintain memory, connections, and application state over time. |

In conclusion, CI/CD environments are fundamentally misaligned with the requirements of a 24/7 agent workload. The hard runtime limits on hosted runners make continuous execution impossible without introducing unreliability. Self-hosted runners, while offering more configurability, shift the burden of management and maintenance to the user and carry a severe risk of account suspension due to perceived security threats. Neither option provides the stable, persistent, and managed environment necessary for an agent to operate reliably and continuously.

## Cross-Cutting Risks: Terms of Service, Acceptable Use Policies, and Suspension

Beyond the technical and architectural barriers, a critical dimension of evaluating the suitability of any free compute tier for a 24/7 agent workload lies in the legal and policy framework governing its use. Every cloud service operates under a set of Terms of Service (ToS) and an Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) that define permissible and prohibited activities [[108,109]]. These documents are not mere formalities; they are legally binding agreements that platforms enforce rigorously, often through automated systems that can lead to swift and sometimes opaque consequences, including service termination and complete account suspension [[3]]. For a 24/7 agent operating on a free tier, the risk of inadvertently violating these policies is exceptionally high, transforming the endeavor from a technical challenge into a significant compliance hazard.

An Acceptable Use Policy serves as a digital rulebook, outlining how users may and may not interact with a platform's resources [[108]]. Key components typically include prohibitions against unauthorized access, denial-of-service attacks, excessive resource consumption, and the generation of harmful or illegal content [[77]]. The ToS for a service like Serverless AI, for example, incorporates its terms of use directly into the broader services agreement, establishing a clear contractual obligation for the user [[18]]. Similarly, Hugging Face's Content Policy is explicitly incorporated into its binding Terms of Service, making adherence mandatory for all users, including those operating AI agents [[9]]. These policies are designed to protect the platform provider from abuse, ensure fair resource distribution among legitimate users, and mitigate legal liabilities. An AI agent operating continuously on a free tier would almost certainly engage in activities that fall into a grey area or outright violate these policies. For instance, an agent consuming its entire daily quota on ZeroGPU [[44]] or running a job on a CI runner for its maximum allowed duration [[13]] could be interpreted by automated systems as an attempt to "monetize" the free service or otherwise abuse the system's intended purpose.

The enforcement of these policies is a primary concern. Platforms like GitHub have been documented to suspend accounts for alleged misuse of their free services, even in cases where the user claims innocence [[5]]. In one documented case, a developer's personal account was suspended for Actions misuse despite the repositories containing no workflows at all, suggesting that automated abuse detectors flagging unusual account-level behavior were the trigger [[5]]. Another user reported receiving a complete account suspension with zero detail or human response, highlighting the lack of transparency in the enforcement process [[6]]. GitHub's stated position is that bans are not random but are triggered by specific rules, algorithms, or automated detectors that flag something as "off" [[3]]. This creates a precarious situation for anyone attempting to push the boundaries of a free tier. An agent designed to run 24/7 would exhibit a constant, uninterrupted pattern of resource consumption—one that is entirely unnatural for the bursty, intermittent usage patterns these free tiers are designed to accommodate. Such behavior would be highly conspicuous to automated monitoring systems, making it nearly inevitable that the account would be flagged for "misuse" or "abuse." The consequence would likely be the termination of the free service and, potentially, a permanent ban from the platform, erasing all progress and investment made.

The table below summarizes the cross-cutting risks associated with using free compute tiers for a 24/7 agent workload.

| Risk Category | Description | Potential Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Terms of Service Violation** | Operating a 24/7 agent likely constitutes "misuse" or "abuse" by consuming resources continuously, contrary to the intended on-demand model [[3]]. | Termination of the free service, potential account suspension. |
| **Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)** | Activities like monopolizing resources, exceeding quotas, or exhibiting unusual network behavior can violate the AUP [[58,76]]. | Automated flagging, service restrictions, or account termination. |
| **Account Suspension** | Automated systems may trigger suspensions for perceived abuse, often with little to no warning or explanation [[5,6]]. | Complete loss of access to the platform and all associated projects. |
| **Legal and Compliance Risk** | Any content generated or actions taken by the agent must comply with the platform's policies, which may be difficult to guarantee [[9]]. | Legal liability, takedown of the agent, and platform-level sanctions. |

Ultimately, the legal and policy landscape presents a formidable barrier that is independent of the technical limitations. Even if a hypothetical loophole existed to circumvent technical limits, the high probability of violating the platform's ToS and AUP makes the use of free tiers for 24/7 agent workloads an exercise in regulatory risk. The combination of opaque enforcement mechanisms and the natural suspicion that arises from observing a user who is not "using the service as intended" creates a high likelihood of punitive action from the provider. This compliance risk is a decisive factor that should disqualify any of these platforms as viable options for supporting a persistent, mission-critical agent.

## Architectural Mismatch: The Core Conflict Between Agentic Workloads and Modern Cloud Models

At the heart of the unsuitability of serverless GPUs, Hugging Face ZeroGPU, and CI runners for 24/7 agent workloads lies a fundamental architectural mismatch. The problem is not merely a matter of insufficient quotas or hard runtime limits; it is a deeper, philosophical and structural conflict between the nature of agentic workloads and the economic models that drive modern cloud infrastructure. Agentic workloads are characterized by persistence, statefulness, and continuous resource consumption [[23]]. An effective AI agent needs a stable, always-on environment to maintain its internal state, manage its memory, orchestrate its tools, and respond to external stimuli in real-time. This requirement for a persistent host is the defining feature of an agent. In stark contrast, the platforms evaluated in this report are architected around the principles of ephemerality, statelessness, and event-driven, pay-per-use economics [[54,75]]. They are built to optimize for idle resource costs, which is the exact opposite of an agent's need for constant availability.

The "scale-to-zero" paradigm is the most salient example of this architectural divergence. This model, employed by serverless GPU platforms and CI runners alike, dictates that compute resources are provisioned only when needed and de-provisioned when they are not [[75]]. For a demo app on ZeroGPU that sees heavy traffic during business hours and none at night, this model is ideal—it maximizes resource utilization and minimizes cost [[33]]. For a CI job that only runs once a day on a scheduled cron trigger, it ensures that expensive compute hardware is not left idling uselessly [[69]]. However, for a 24/7 agent, this paradigm is fatal. The agent's entire purpose depends on having a continuous, uninterrupted session. Every time the platform scales the GPU down to zero because the agent's activity dips below a certain threshold, the agent's state is destroyed, and it must undergo a costly and disruptive restart process. This constant cycle of spinning up and shutting down is antithetical to the goal of reliable, persistent operation. The agent would be engaged in a perpetual battle against its own infrastructure, fighting to stay alive and maintain its context.

This conflict extends to the very definition of a "job" or "task." In CI/CD and many serverless contexts, a job is a finite, stateless unit of work. It takes input, performs a calculation, and produces an output before terminating. An AI agent, however, is not a job; it is a service. It does not "complete" in the traditional sense. Its lifecycle is open-ended, defined by its programming and its environment. The platforms' reliance on event-driven triggers (e.g., a push to a repository, a webhook call) is also ill-suited for an agent that must proactively monitor its environment and initiate actions based on internal logic or external cues. While workarounds exist, such as configuring a CI job to poll an API in a loop, this approach is inefficient, consumes resources unnecessarily, and is fragile. It transforms the agent into a pseudo-job, losing the benefits of a true persistent service.

The economic models reinforce this architectural divide. GPU clouds and serverless platforms were built to compete with hyperscalers by offering significantly lower prices for specific types of workloads [[74]]. Their profitability hinges on achieving high utilization rates for their expensive hardware. The "scale-to-zero" model is a key enabler of this strategy. By keeping GPUs powered down when not in use, providers can serve more customers with the same physical hardware. An agent running 24/7 on a single free GPU would represent a massive inefficiency for the provider from a utilization standpoint. It occupies a valuable resource continuously without contributing to the high-throughput, bursty traffic patterns that these systems are optimized to handle. This is why free tiers are invariably limited—they are marketing tools, not sustainable business models for providing free, persistent compute. The platforms are designed to extract value from users through paid tiers, and any attempt to subvert this model by maximizing free resource consumption is viewed as abuse [[3]].

In essence, attempting to run a 24/7 agent on these platforms is like trying to run a database on a serverless function. While it is possible to write code that mimics the desired behavior, the underlying architecture prevents it from functioning reliably or efficiently. The agent requires persistence, but the platform is built for impermanence. The agent requires a stable state, but the platform is stateless by design. The agent requires continuous access to resources, but the platform is built for intermittent, on-demand access. This profound architectural mismatch means that no amount of clever coding or configuration can overcome the fundamental limitations imposed by the platform's core design principles. The solution to the problem of hosting an agent is not to shoehorn it into an inappropriate environment, but to select an infrastructure model that is purpose-built for the task.

## Final Verdict: An Unviable Combination of Limitations and Risks

After a comprehensive analysis of the landscape of free compute tiers—including general serverless GPU platforms, Hugging Face Spaces with ZeroGPU, and CI runner environments (GitHub Actions, GitLab Runners)—the final verdict is unequivocal: none of these options are suitable for supporting a 24/7 agent workload. The attempt to deploy a persistent, stateful agent on these platforms is doomed to fail due to a confluence of insurmountable technical limitations, fundamental architectural mismatches, and significant compliance risks. Each category of service is built on a paradigm that is directly opposed to the continuous, uninterrupted operation required by an AI agent.

Serverless GPU platforms are engineered for on-demand, bursty workloads and scale-to-zero architectures [[54,75]]. Their free tiers consist of limited credits or quotas that are wholly inadequate for sustained operation and are designed as introductory offers, not long-term solutions [[20,22]]. Attempting to run an agent continuously would be interpreted by the platform's economics as inefficient and abusive, leading to a high risk of service termination and account suspension [[3]]. Hugging Face's ZeroGPU is similarly architected against persistent processes, operating on a strict daily time quota (as low as 3.5 minutes for free users) and a dynamic, first-come-first-served allocation system [[33,44]]. An agent would be perpetually blocked behind a queue or exhausted by its quota, making continuous functionality impossible [[35]]. Furthermore, its activities are governed by an Acceptable Use Policy, creating legal and operational risks [[9]]. Finally, CI runner environments are fundamentally event-driven and job-oriented [[68]]. They impose hard, non-negotiable runtime limits (e.g., 6 hours on GitHub Actions) that would force a 24/7 agent into a constant and unreliable cycle of restarting [[13,69]]. Self-hosted runners, while seemingly more flexible, introduce prohibitive management overhead and carry a severe risk of automatic suspension for triggering security alerts [[1,72]].

The core issue transcends individual platform policies and points to a deeper conflict between the nature of agentic workloads and the prevailing economic model of modern cloud computing. Agentic workloads demand persistence, statefulness, and dedicated resources, whereas these free tiers are predicated on ephemerality, statelessness, and shared, on-demand resources [[23,75]]. This architectural mismatch makes the proposed use case fundamentally untenable. The investigation confirms that achieving reliable, uninterrupted uptime for an AI agent necessitates paid, appropriately provisioned infrastructure, such as dedicated GPU instances or specialized serverless endpoint services that are designed to accommodate persistent workloads [[59,103]].
