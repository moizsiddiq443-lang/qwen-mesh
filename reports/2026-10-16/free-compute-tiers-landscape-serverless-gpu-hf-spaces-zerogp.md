# The Unavailability of Free 24/7 Compute: A Landscape Analysis of Serverless, Demo, and CI Platforms

This report provides a deep research analysis of the landscape of free-tier compute resources suitable for deploying continuous, 24/7 AI agent workloads. The investigation focuses on three distinct categories of platforms: general-purpose serverless GPU providers, Hugging Face Spaces' ZeroGPU service, and the CI/CD runner services offered by GitHub Actions and GitLab. The evaluation is grounded in a rigorous examination of technical specifications, operational constraints, business models, and, critically, the terms of service governing the use of these platforms. The core objective is to determine whether any genuinely free infrastructure exists that can reliably and sustainably support the persistent, stateful, and often long-running nature of autonomous AI agents. Each section deconstructs the suitability of a specific category, analyzing its architectural design, resource allocation, quota systems, and inherent limitations. The final synthesis consolidates these findings to provide a clear, evidence-based assessment of the current state of free compute for agentic workloads.

## Serverless GPU Platforms: A Mismatch of Architecture and Requirement

Serverless GPU platforms, including prominent services like Modal, RunPod, Baseten, and Koyeb, have emerged as powerful abstractions for managing GPU-intensive machine learning workloads [[9]]. These platforms are built on a fundamental architecture of scale-to-zero, where compute resources are provisioned on-demand and automatically scaled down to zero when idle, eliminating costs during periods of inactivity [[15,38]]. While this model offers significant advantages for sporadic or bursty workloads, it exhibits a profound mismatch with the requirements of a continuous, 24/7 AI agent. The very design principles that make them efficient for short-lived inference jobs or batch processing introduce prohibitive costs, unacceptable performance penalties, and architectural complexities that render them unsuitable for persistent agentic operation.

The economic model of serverless platforms is predicated on the assumption of variable traffic patterns [[34]]. For workloads with spiky demand, the ability to scale to zero means users only pay for the seconds their code is actively executing, leading to substantial cost savings compared to maintaining a perpetually running, dedicated GPU instance [[15]]. However, this advantage evaporates when applied to a workload that never scales down. An AI agent operating continuously must constantly contend with the "cold start" penalty, which is the time and compute cost associated with provisioning a new container, loading dependencies, and initializing models before it can begin processing requests [[15]]. RunPod's analysis illustrates this point starkly: for a workload running at 100% utilization, its serverless model becomes significantly more expensive than its on-demand Secure Cloud instances, with the break-even point for cost-effectiveness occurring around 66% utilization [[39]]. This implies that a 24/7 agent would incur substantially higher costs than a comparable task on a dedicated or "always-on" pod, which bills per second for active compute without the overhead of repeated initialization cycles [[28,39]].

This economic inefficiency is directly tied to the high latency introduced by cold starts, a critical failure point for many agentic applications. Every time a serverless function is invoked after a period of inactivity, it incurs a startup delay while the runtime environment is prepared [[15]]. While leading platforms have invested heavily in mitigating this issue through advanced technologies, the problem remains a central challenge. Modal, for example, employs a sophisticated combination of CPU memory snapshotting via gVisor and CUDA checkpoint/restore capabilities to dramatically reduce boot times, cutting them from several minutes down to tens of seconds for large language model servers [[41]]. Similarly, Beam claims 2-3 second cold starts by using a lazy-loading container runtime, and RunPod utilizes a feature called FlashBoot to achieve sub-second startups in some cases [[10,34]]. Despite these advancements, even sub-second latencies are often unacceptable for real-time interactions or complex reasoning loops within an agent. Replicate highlights the severity of this issue on its platform, noting that custom deployments can experience cold starts exceeding 60 seconds, making it wholly unsuitable for latency-sensitive applications [[35,49]]. For an agent whose functionality depends on seamless, uninterrupted processing, the multi-second delay imposed by a frequent cold start would likely render it non-functional.

Furthermore, the ephemeral nature of serverless functions presents a significant architectural hurdle for stateful agents. Most serverless platforms, including Modal, are designed to be stateless; any data stored within a function's local filesystem or memory is lost once the function instance terminates [[33,40]]. This forces developers to externalize all state management to separate, dedicated services. A common production pattern involves a three-tier memory architecture: using a managed Redis service like Upstash for session-level working memory, a vector database like Qdrant for long-term semantic recall, and a relational database like PostgreSQL for structured history logging [[28,30]]. While this separation of concerns is a robust architectural practice, it fundamentally complicates deployment and increases operational overhead. It negates one of the primary value propositions of serverless—abstracting away infrastructure management—and reintroduces the DevOps complexity that practitioners sought to avoid by adopting these platforms [[29]]. Some platforms attempt to address this with persistent volumes, but these often come with additional costs and may not persist across different provider ecosystems, creating vendor lock-in [[33,38]].

The product-market fit for these platforms is also questionable. Many of the top serverless GPU providers explicitly state they are not designed for persistent services. Modal's documentation clarifies that its serverless offering is optimized for stateless Python functions and workflows, and it lacks native support for persistent services, API endpoints, or background workers [[12]]. Baseten similarly has no support for background workers, limiting its utility for agentic applications that require long-running processes [[12]]. This indicates that the core offerings are intentionally architected against the kind of continuous execution needed by agents. Even when features like persistent services are available, as on RunPod, they operate under a different pricing model (billed for both compute and storage) and are better described as "pods" rather than traditional serverless functions, signaling a different class of service altogether [[28]].

Finally, the free tiers offered by these platforms, while appearing generous at first glance, are clearly intended for short, bursty workloads and are insufficient for continuous operation. Modal's free tier provides $30 in monthly credits, but this budget is quickly consumed by any persistent application [[33,41]]. A simple persistent service will continue to consume these credits until it is explicitly stopped, meaning a developer cannot rely on a recurring, automatic credit allocation for an always-on agent [[33]]. The free tier effectively serves as a sandbox for experimentation with short-lived jobs, not as a foundation for a production-grade, 24/7 agent. RunPod does not offer a default free tier, instead providing one-time signup credits, which are also inadequate for sustained operation [[10,11]]. In essence, serverless GPU platforms represent a fundamental misalignment between their core value proposition and the demands of continuous AI agent workloads. Their architecture introduces prohibitive costs and performance bottlenecks, while their product roadmaps and free tier offerings confirm their focus on intermittent, rather than perpetual, execution.

## Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU: A System Designed for Sporadic Use

Hugging Face's ZeroGPU service stands apart from other free compute options due to its unique hardware offering and innovative just-in-time (JIT) allocation model [[4,8]]. Powered by NVIDIA H200 GPUs, it provides access to enterprise-class hardware that is otherwise difficult to obtain on a free basis [[2,11]]. However, this very innovation, combined with stringent quota limitations, makes it fundamentally incompatible with the needs of a 24/7 AI agent workload. The system is architecturally and commercially designed for sporadic, interactive use cases like demos, classroom labs, and hackathons, where usage is brief and infrequent [[4]].

The core principle of ZeroGPU is dynamic, on-demand allocation. A GPU slice is not permanently assigned to an application; instead, it is allocated only when a user interacts with a Space and triggers a function decorated with `@spaces.GPU` [[1,7]]. As soon as the computation completes, the GPU resource is released back into a shared pool, making it available for other users [[4,6]]. This JIT model is highly efficient for resource utilization, ensuring that expensive GPU capacity is only consumed during actual compute phases and is completely free during periods of inactivity [[7]]. This design is antithetical to the concept of a persistent agent, which by definition requires a continuous, uninterrupted stream of computational resources to maintain its state and perform its tasks. An agent's constant activity would keep the GPU allocated indefinitely, defeating the purpose of the shared, elastic infrastructure and placing a disproportionate burden on the system, a scenario explicitly discouraged by platform policies [[22]].

The most significant barrier to using ZeroGPU for a 24/7 agent is its strict, time-based quota system. For unauthenticated users, the daily quota is a mere two minutes [[1,2]]. For authenticated free accounts, this increases slightly to either 3.5 or 5 minutes, depending on the source, but remains extremely limited [[2,8,13]]. Crucially, this quota resets exactly 24 hours after the first use of the day, meaning an agent would be forced offline every single day, destroying any continuity of thought or state [[1,8]]. Even Pro subscribers, who receive a much larger daily quota of 25 to 40 minutes, are expected to manage their consumption carefully, indicating that the system is not intended for unfettered, continuous operation [[1,7]]. The introduction of a separate, request-based limit restricting free users to three runs per day further cements its position as a tool for infrequent interaction rather than a platform for persistent services [[5]]. Attempting to run an agent that requires more than a few minutes of compute time per day would necessitate upgrading to a paid plan, which offers pre-paid credits at a rate of $1 per 10 minutes of additional GPU time [[7,8]].

Beyond the quota limitations, ZeroGPU imposes significant technical constraints that hinder its use for complex ML applications. It exclusively supports the Gradio SDK (v4+), PyTorch versions from 2.8.0 to the latest, and specific Python versions (3.10 or 3.12) [[1,7]]. More importantly, the platform's short-lived process model makes standard optimization techniques inefficient. Just-in-time compilation with `torch.compile` is particularly ineffective because the compiled artifacts cannot be reused across the multiple, short-lived processes that handle requests, forcing developers to rely on slower ahead-of-time (AoT) compilation or filesystem caches [[1,6]]. This adds another layer of performance overhead, counteracting the efficiency gains that might be expected from a modern ML stack. Furthermore, certain optimizations like `torch.compile` are simply not supported [[1]]. The error messages related to quota exhaustion can also be ambiguous, sometimes returning the same message for both time-based and request-based limits being exceeded, which can complicate debugging for developers trying to optimize their agent's resource consumption [[3,5]].

In summary, Hugging Face's ZeroGPU is a powerful tool for its intended purpose: enabling anyone to experiment with and deploy interactive AI demos powered by high-end GPUs without upfront cost. Its value lies in its accessibility and the quality of the underlying hardware. However, its architectural design, centered on a JIT allocation model and severe daily quotas, makes it entirely unsuitable for hosting a persistent, 24/7 AI agent. Using it for such a purpose would constitute a misuse of the system, akin to attempting to power a factory assembly line with a handheld power drill. The platform's design prioritizes resource efficiency and fair sharing among a large number of users, a goal that is directly contradicted by the continuous, exclusive resource consumption demanded by an autonomous agent.

## CI/CD Runner Services: A Minefield of Policy and Technical Barriers

Using CI/CD runner services, such as GitHub Actions and GitLab CI, to host a 24/7 AI agent workload places a practitioner in a precarious and high-risk position. These platforms are governed by explicit Terms of Service that prohibit the very activity of running a persistent server-like task, and they possess hard technical limitations that make indefinite operation impossible. While they offer substantial free compute allowances, leveraging them for agentic purposes constitutes "resource abuse" and carries a significant threat of account suspension and termination [[22,24]].

The most immediate and critical barrier is the Terms of Service. Both GitHub and GitLab explicitly forbid using their CI/CD platforms for activities that place a disproportionate burden on their servers relative to the user's benefit [[22,25]]. The examples cited in GitHub's ToS include using Actions as a content delivery network (CDN) or as part of a serverless application, which perfectly describes the intent of running an AI agent [[22]]. GitHub's automated systems are specifically trained to detect long-running network processes, scripts that attempt to keep runners alive, and unusual network usage patterns—all common characteristics of a persistent agent [[24]]. Multiple reports confirm that GitHub has actively suspended accounts for violations linked to AI agents, often citing vague reasons like "suspicious activity" or "violations of our Terms of Service" without providing specific details, leaving users in the dark [[26,68]]. In one notable case, access was disabled for an entire open-source organization due to actions by "drive-by contributors" suspected of crypto-mining, highlighting how easily an account can be tainted by association [[69]]. This creates an unstable and unpredictable operating environment where a developer's work could be deleted overnight with little recourse [[26]].

Even if one were to ignore the policy risks, these platforms impose insurmountable technical barriers to continuous operation. GitHub Actions enforces a hard cap of 6 hours for a single job on its free plan [[16,20]]. GitLab's shared runners have a default maximum execution time of 1 hour [[47]]. Since many agentic workflows involve long-running loops, WebSocket connections, or complex reasoning chains, these limits are frequently exceeded, causing the agent's process to be abruptly terminated. While self-hosted runners can be configured to bypass the 6-hour limit, they introduce significant security vulnerabilities and maintenance burdens, as they require the user to manage their own infrastructure, patch systems, and secure against attacks [[17,31]]. The upcoming introduction of a $0.002 per minute platform fee for all self-hosted runner usage, effective March 1, 2026, further erodes the financial appeal of this approach, transforming what was once a free alternative into a metered service [[18,19,32]].

The default architecture of hosted runners on both platforms is also fundamentally incompatible with persistence. On GitLab.com, for example, each job runs in a fully ephemeral, dedicated VM that is destroyed immediately after execution [[21]]. This model ensures perfect isolation and reproducibility for build and test tasks but is fatal for an agent that needs to maintain state and run continuously. Any processes or data created by the agent would be wiped out as soon as the job finished. While self-hosted runners can persist, they become a permanent fixture of the user's own cloud or on-premises infrastructure, defeating the purpose of utilizing a "free tier." Furthermore, the execution of free shared runners is often subject to long queue times due to high demand, introducing unpredictable delays that are detrimental to an agent's responsiveness [[50]].

The billing models themselves add another layer of complexity and risk. GitHub's free allowance of 2,000 Linux-equivalent minutes does not roll over and is consumed at different rates depending on the runner type; Windows jobs count as 2x minutes and macOS jobs as 10x minutes, which can lead to unexpected quota exhaustion [[20]]. GitLab uses a "cost factor" system where the effective quota consumption varies dramatically based on project visibility, with forks receiving a much larger initial quota than the main project, creating a confusing and potentially exploitable system [[45,46]]. For a developer attempting to run a continuous agent, the combination of unpredictable execution timing, potential for quota overage, and the ever-present threat of account suspension makes CI/CD platforms a deeply flawed and unreliable choice for this use case.

In conclusion, the use of CI/CD runner services for hosting a 24/7 AI agent is not a viable strategy. It represents a direct violation of platform policies, invites a high risk of catastrophic account loss, and is technically constrained by hard limits on job duration and the ephemeral nature of the default infrastructure. The free compute minutes offered are a secondary concern compared to the existential risk posed by the terms of service and the fundamental architectural mismatch.

## Comparative Analysis of Resource Allocation and Quotas

A detailed comparison of the resource allocation and quota systems across the analyzed platforms reveals a clear distinction between those designed for intermittent use and those that, in theory, could support longer tasks. However, even the platforms with seemingly generous allowances are ultimately constrained by policies and technicalities that make them unsuitable for a truly 24/7 workload. This analysis focuses on quantifiable metrics such as GPU hardware, vCPU/RAM ratios, VRAM size, and the specifics of free tier quotas.

For serverless GPU platforms, the hardware offerings vary widely, but the billing model remains consistent. RunPod provides a broad spectrum of GPUs, from consumer-grade RTX cards to enterprise-class H100 and H200 models, with corresponding vCPU and RAM allocations for each class [[38]]. For instance, its 80GB class includes an H100 GPU with 16 vCPUs and 188GB RAM, while its 141GB class offers an H200 GPU [[38]]. Modal aggregates capacity from partners like Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, offering access to A100, H100, and even B300 GPUs, abstracting the underlying hardware selection from the developer [[14,42]]. Baseten also supports a wide range of GPUs including T4, L4, A100, and H100 [[10]]. The key limitation here is the lack of a default free tier on most platforms; RunPod offers a one-time $10 signup credit, and Modal's free tier is a recurring $30/month credit for compute, neither of which is sufficient for continuous operation [[11,33]]. The quotas are therefore not time-based in the traditional sense but are instead tied to the consumption of these credits.

Hugging Face's ZeroGPU stands out for offering access to NVIDIA H200 GPUs, which are among the most powerful consumer and professional cards available [[4,8]]. The platform dynamically allocates an MIG slice of an H200, exposing approximately 70 GB of VRAM for a 'large' instance or up to 141 GB for an 'xlarge' instance [[4,6,8]]. This is a significant resource, especially considering the price point. However, as previously established, access is controlled by a severe daily quota system. Free accounts receive between 3.5 and 5 minutes of GPU time per day, which resets every 24 hours [[1,2,8]]. Using the 'xlarge' instance, which consumes double the quota, would halve the available time to just 1.75 to 2.5 minutes [[1,2]]. This quota structure is explicitly designed to prevent sustained use and ensure fairness for a large user base engaged in sporadic, interactive tasks.

CI/CD platforms measure their free tiers in compute minutes rather than GPU-hours. GitHub's free plan for public repositories offers an unlimited allowance, while private repositories receive 2,000 Linux-equivalent minutes per month [[20]]. This quota is not rolled over and is consumed at a 2x rate for Windows jobs and a 10x rate for macOS jobs, making cross-platform usage expensive [[20]]. GitLab provides a flat 400 minutes per month on its shared runners [[50]]. The critical constraint is not the total number of minutes but the hard limit on a single job's execution time. GitHub caps this at 6 hours, and GitLab's shared runners at 1 hour [[16,47]]. These limits are frequently insufficient for the complex, multi-step reasoning loops that characterize many AI agents, guaranteeing that any long-running task will be terminated prematurely.

No single option provides the combination of continuous availability, sufficient resource allocation, and policy compliance necessary for a reliable free-tier solution.

## State Management and Persistence in Free-Tier Environments

A critical requirement for any functional AI agent is the ability to maintain state across sessions and over extended periods. This involves managing working memory for the current task, recalling information from past interactions, and logging actions for future reference. The platforms analyzed in this report present a fragmented and challenging landscape for state management, often forcing developers to construct a multi-service stack using various free-tier offerings to compensate for the limitations of the primary compute platform. The ephemeral nature of serverless and CI runner environments is a primary driver of this complexity.

Most serverless GPU platforms are inherently stateless, meaning any data generated or stored within a function's execution environment is transient and lost once the function completes [[40]]. Modal, for instance, provides `modal.Volume` objects for persistent storage, but these are treated as separate, attached resources rather than integrated parts of the compute instance [[33]]. The data persists even if the function is terminated, allowing it to be loaded upon subsequent invocations, but this adds another component to manage and incurs additional costs for the volume storage itself [[38]]. This architectural pattern of separating compute from state is sound but shifts the operational burden onto the developer. For an agent requiring a durable runtime—a workflow engine that can preserve progress, coordinate parallel work, and resume execution after waiting for external events—the developer must orchestrate this logic externally, often using a combination of a task queue and a database [[44]]. This is precisely why specialized platforms like Northflank and Render are gaining traction; they offer integrated solutions for persistent state, such as managed Postgres databases with pgvector support and persistent disks, which are essential for multi-agent systems [[29]].

When relying on free-tier services, the choices for external state management are limited but viable. For short-term, in-memory state, a managed Redis service is ideal. Upstash offers a generous free tier that includes 500,000 Redis commands per month and 256 MB of storage [[57,58]]. This is sufficient for handling a moderate number of state updates and queries for an agent's working memory, though the command limit could be a bottleneck for a high-frequency agent [[55]]. The HTTP-based API of Upstash also makes it compatible with edge runtimes that may not support native TCP connections [[55]].

For long-term, semantic memory, a vector database is necessary. Qdrant Cloud provides a permanent free tier that includes a single-node cluster with 0.5 vCPU, 1 GB of RAM, and 4 GB of disk space [[59,61]]. This configuration is adequate for storing and querying approximately one million 768-dimensional vectors, which can serve as a knowledge base for an agent [[60]]. However, the free tier comes with a best-effort SLA and an automatic suspension policy: the cluster is suspended after one week of inactivity and permanently deleted after four weeks if not reactivated [[59]]. While this is less restrictive than a quota reset, it still poses a risk for an agent that might need to pause for maintenance or debugging. Furthermore, the storage capacity is limited; a free cluster can hold about 250,000 uncompressed 1,536-dimension vectors, which could be exhausted in days of active use [[61]].

For structured historical logging of all agent actions, a relational database is the standard solution. Neon offers a free tier with 0.5 GB of storage and 100 compute-unit hours per month [[62,64]]. Its key feature is "scale-to-zero" compute, which suspends the database after five minutes of inactivity, saving costs [[64]]. However, this same feature is a major drawback for a 24/7 agent, as it would cause the database to go to sleep and incur a cold start delay of 500ms to 2 seconds whenever the agent tries to log a new event [[64,65]]. This latency is often unacceptable for real-time applications. Moreover, the compute-hour allowance is quickly consumed by a constantly running database, making the free tier economically unsustainable for continuous operation [[65]].

Ultimately, building a persistent agent on free tiers requires a composite architecture. A developer might use a self-hosted runner for the main compute loop and pair it with Upstash for hot data, Qdrant for vector search, and perhaps a self-hosted PostgreSQL instance on a cheap VPS for logs. This approach is complex, fragile, and dependent on the continued stability of multiple third-party services. It highlights a significant gap in the market: the lack of a single, integrated platform that combines affordable or free persistent compute with robust, scalable state management tools.

## Synthesis and Practical Recommendations

The comprehensive analysis of free compute tiers from serverless GPU platforms, Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU, and CI/CD runner services leads to a definitive conclusion: there is no single, officially sanctioned, and reliable free tier capable of hosting a 24/7 AI agent workload. Each category of platform is fundamentally misaligned with the core requirements of persistence, continuous operation, and state management demanded by autonomous agents. The pursuit of a cost-free solution in this domain is fraught with technical limitations, prohibitive costs for sustained use, and significant policy risks.

Serverless GPU platforms like Modal and RunPod are architecturally optimized for bursty, intermittent traffic patterns [[14,15]]. Their scale-to-zero model, while economical for sporadic tasks, becomes highly inefficient and expensive for a workload that never scales down [[39]]. The persistent cold-start latency, even with technological mitigations, poses a performance bottleneck for real-time agentic tasks [[41,49]]. Furthermore, their stateless nature and lack of native support for persistent services force developers into complex, multi-service architectures that negate the simplicity promised by serverless abstraction [[12,30]].

Hugging Face's ZeroGPU service, despite its powerful H200 GPU hardware, is explicitly designed for sporadic, interactive use cases [[4,8]]. Its just-in-time allocation model and severely limited daily quotas (as low as 5 minutes for free users) make continuous operation impossible without payment or violating system policies [[1,2]]. The platform's design is antithetical to the continuous resource consumption of an agent.

CI/CD runner services from GitHub and GitLab represent a minefield of policy and technical barriers. Their Terms of Service explicitly prohibit using the platforms for "server-like" tasks or any activity deemed to place an undue burden on their infrastructure, a category that unequivocally includes running a 24/7 agent [[22,24]]. This creates a high risk of sudden and unannounced account suspension [[26,69]]. Technically, hard limits on job duration (6 hours on GitHub, 1 hour on GitLab) and the ephemeral nature of hosted runners make them fundamentally unsuited for uninterrupted, stateful processes [[16,21,47]].

Given this reality, practitioners seeking to deploy an AI agent without cost should consider the following strategic pathways, understanding that each involves significant trade-offs and risks:

1.  **Embrace Specialized Free Tiers:** Instead of repurposing general-purpose platforms, focus on newer, more specialized services designed for the exact use case. Platforms like Northflank and Render are emerging as strong contenders, offering free tiers that include persistent services with real CPU/RAM and integrated state management capabilities [[29,52]]. This path is more promising than attempting to shoehorn existing services into a role they were not designed for.

2.  **Build a Composite Solution (High Effort):** For experienced developers comfortable with infrastructure management, a viable but complex approach involves combining multiple free-tier services. This could entail using a self-hosted runner for the main compute loop (accepting the security and maintenance overhead) and pairing it with free tiers from services like Qdrant Cloud for vector memory and Upstash Redis for working memory [[28,58,59]]. This approach requires meticulous management and is brittle, as the entire system depends on the stable policies and continued free offerings of multiple third parties.

3.  **Utilize Notebook-Based Environments for Development:** Platforms like Google Colab and Kaggle Notebooks offer free GPU access for development and testing [[11]]. While their session limits (up to 12 hours) and reliance on idle timeouts make them unsuitable for production hosting, they are excellent for iterating on an agent's logic, fine-tuning models, and validating components in a GPU-equipped environment before attempting deployment.

4.  **Accept Paid, Purpose-Built Infrastructure:** The most reliable and least risky path is to accept that robust, persistent compute for AI agents is a paid service. Investing in a small, always-on instance on a platform like RunPod, or using the free trials and then scaling to a paid plan on Fly.io or Render, ensures reliability, performance, and compliance with all terms of service [[29,51]]. The cost of a few dollars per month for a dedicated, persistent virtual machine is a fraction of the risk and effort involved in cobbling together a free-tier solution.

In summary, the promise of a free, 24/7 compute solution for AI agents remains largely unfulfilled by mainstream providers. The most pragmatic recommendation is to leverage the free tiers of platforms specifically designed for this purpose, such as Render or Northflank, or to accept the modest cost of a dedicated, always-on virtual machine to ensure the agent operates reliably and without violation of platform policies.
