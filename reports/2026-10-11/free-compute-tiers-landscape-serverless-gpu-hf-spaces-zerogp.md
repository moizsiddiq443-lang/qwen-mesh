# Beyond the Free Tier: Why Persistent AI Agents Cannot Operate Without Paid Compute

This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the landscape of free-tier compute resources, evaluating their suitability for sustaining continuous, 24/7 AI agent workloads. The investigation focuses on three distinct categories of infrastructure: Hugging Face Spaces' ZeroGPU offering, general-purpose serverless GPU platforms, and the repurposed compute capacity of Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment (CI/CD) runner systems from GitHub and GitLab. The evaluation prioritizes technical specifications, inherent limitations, business model shifts, and policy constraints to determine the practical feasibility of maintaining uninterrupted agent execution within advertised free tiers. The analysis concludes by examining emerging sandboxing technologies and synthesizing findings into a definitive assessment of the current market's ability to support free, persistent agent hosting.

## The Infeasibility of Continuous Operation on Quota-Limited Infrastructure: A Deep Dive into Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU

The Hugging Face ecosystem, particularly its Spaces platform, presents a compelling entry point for developers seeking free compute resources, prominently featuring the "ZeroGPU" option [[32]]. However, a detailed examination reveals that its architectural design is fundamentally misaligned with the operational requirements of a 24/7 AI agent. ZeroGPU operates on a strict, quota-based system designed for intermittent, event-driven usage rather than continuous operation, rendering it entirely unsuitable for sustaining a persistent background process [[2,24]]. Its core principle is cost efficiency through non-persistence; a GPU is allocated only when an application's compute function is actively running and is immediately released upon completion or a brief period of idleness [[2,5]]. This "just-in-time" (JIT) model makes it ideal for demos, side projects, and low-traffic applications but creates insurmountable barriers for any workload requiring guaranteed availability and responsiveness [[2]].

The most significant impediment to using ZeroGPU for a 24/7 agent is its stringent daily time quota. For free personal accounts, the daily allowance of GPU time is exceptionally limited, with sources reporting figures ranging from 2 to 5 minutes per day [[3,8,26]]. One source specifies a more precise figure of 3.5 minutes for authenticated users [[7]], while another states 5 minutes [[28]]. This quota resets exactly 24 hours after the first GPU usage session of the day [[7,8,26]]. To put this in perspective, even a single hour of continuous agent execution would require 12 to 30 days' worth of free quota, making sustained operation impossible. The situation for PRO subscribers ($9/month) is significantly better, with a quota of up to 40 minutes per day [[3,24]], but this is still insufficient for a full 24-hour cycle and positions the feature as a paid enhancement rather than a free solution. The quota system is further complicated by its consumption mechanics. The platform consumes quota based on the *requested* duration of a function call, not the actual time the GPU is active [[6]]. By default, any function decorated with `@spaces.GPU` without an explicit duration requests a 60-second reservation [[6,9]]. This means that a simple inference call that completes in milliseconds will still consume a full 60 seconds of the user's daily quota, creating a severe risk of rapid exhaustion [[6]]. Furthermore, the size of the allocated GPU instance affects quota consumption; using the 'xlarge' GPU, which provides a full slice of the card, counts as twice the declared duration in the quota system [[6]]. A May 2026 forum discussion noted that some workloads were automatically falling back to the `xlarge` size after a hardware migration, causing spaces to consume quota at double the expected rate and depleting allowances faster than anticipated [[26]].

Beyond the time-based limit, there appears to be an additional, undocumented layer of restriction. According to a forum post by a key developer, free users are subject to a separate, undocumented daily limit on the number of times they can run a ZeroGPU Space, estimated to be as low as 3 runs per day [[26]]. This request-based limit acts as a second barrier, capable of halting an agent mid-execution if it exceeds the threshold, regardless of remaining time quota. This dual-constraint system—combining a small time pool with a small number of access tokens per day—effectively renders the free tier unusable for any purpose other than very short, infrequent tests [[6,26]]. Even programmatically accessing a space via a client library can trigger different quota allocations; one user reported that using the `gradio_client` was limited to approximately 5 minutes, whereas accessing the space directly in a browser granted around 25 minutes [[4]]. This inconsistency adds another variable that complicates planning and execution for a persistent agent.

The JIT execution model itself introduces critical performance issues for an interactive agent. Each time a function decorated with `@spaces.GPU` is called, a new child process is forked to run the task and is killed immediately upon completion [[5]]. This process involves initialization overhead, including loading models and libraries, which translates into a form of "cold start" latency. While Hugging Face recommends Ahead-of-Time (AoT) compilation using `torch.export` and `AOTInductor` to mitigate this by pre-compiling models and saving them as artifacts, the process of reloading the compiled artifact in a new process still incurs timing penalties [[5]]. Just-in-Time (JIT) compilation with `torch.compile` is explicitly inefficient on ZeroGPU because the compiled model cannot be reused between the short-lived calls [[5]]. For an AI agent that must respond instantly to user input or environmental triggers, this repeated startup delay is a critical flaw that would lead to a poor user experience. The initialization phase can involve downloading large models from a hub, and if not handled correctly with `preload_from_hub`, it can cause the app to fail startup within the default 30-minute timeout [[1]].

Furthermore, the underlying hardware and compatibility present challenges. ZeroGPU dynamically allocates slices of shared NVIDIA GPUs, such as an H200 or RTX Pro 6000 Blackwell [[2,3]]. These are typically Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) slices, which provide isolated portions of a larger GPU but may offer lower performance characteristics compared to a dedicated, full-card instance [[5,9]]. The system also enforces specific software stack requirements, primarily being exclusive to Gradio SDK version 4+ and compatible PyTorch versions (e.g., 2.0.0 to 2.2.0) [[3,9]]. This can create compatibility issues for existing applications not designed with the `@spaces.GPU` decorator pattern, leading to higher failure rates [[25]]. A notable bug related to broader quota exhaustion was identified in older versions of the Gradio SDK, highlighting the need for careful dependency management to avoid unexpected failures [[1]].

Perhaps the most telling indicator of ZeroGPU's unsuitability for serious or commercial use is the strategic shift in Hugging Face's business model. As of late 2026, the ability for free accounts to create new Gradio and Docker Spaces has been revoked, requiring a PRO subscription for this functionality [[25]]. While static Spaces remain free, the evolution of the platform demonstrates a clear intent to move advanced features, including GPU access, toward paid tiers. Free accounts are now limited to hosting up to two ZeroGPU Spaces, provided they have a verified email and an account older than 30 days [[3,25,32]]. This restriction reinforces the perception of the free tier as a hobbyist or transitional offering, not a production-grade solution. The existence of Hugging Face's own paid Inference Endpoints service, which provides dedicated infrastructure with no cold starts and SLA-backed uptime, serves as a direct contrast, confirming that continuous, reliable operation is a premium feature [[8]]. Therefore, while ZeroGPU offers a convenient way to test models with minimal friction, its fundamental design as a quota-limited, JIT resource makes it fundamentally incompatible with the demands of a 24/7 AI agent workload.

## Architectural Mismatches: Why General Serverless GPU Platforms Are Unsuitable for Persistent Agent Workloads

General-purpose serverless GPU platforms, such as Modal, RunPod, Cerebrium, and Northflank, represent a significant advancement in cloud computing, offering on-demand access to powerful GPUs with scale-to-zero capabilities and per-second billing models [[18,22]]. These features promise substantial cost savings for workloads that are not consistently busy. However, despite their flexibility, their core architectural principles are optimized for bursty, asynchronous, or event-driven tasks, making them inherently ill-suited for the continuous, stateful, and low-latency requirements of a 24/7 AI agent. The primary mismatch lies in the concepts of cold starts, execution time limits, and the economic realities of high utilization.

The most prominent architectural flaw for persistent agents is the unavoidable latency penalty of cold starts. When a serverless function is invoked for the first time or after a period of inactivity, the platform must provision and initialize the underlying container or virtual machine, a process that takes time [[29]]. While providers have made remarkable progress in reducing this latency, with leading platforms like Beam achieving sub-10-second cold starts and some functions booting in 2-4 seconds, this delay remains unacceptable for an interactive agent [[27,30]]. For context, a coding agent might face over two minutes of initialization overhead just to clone a large repository before it can begin its primary task, a scenario that highlights the fundamental conflict between serverless design and agent responsiveness [[29]]. Although some platforms offer features to mitigate this, such as Modal's alpha GPU memory snapshots or pre-warmed instances, these are often complex to implement and add another layer of cost and operational overhead [[23,27]]. For a truly free solution, such advanced features are unlikely to be available, leaving the agent vulnerable to repeated initialization delays with every interaction.

Even on platforms that allow for longer-running jobs, there are hard-coded maximum execution time limits that prevent indefinite operation. For example, Modal Sandboxes, while more flexible than traditional serverless functions, have a native maximum lifetime of 24 hours [[46,48]]. After this period, the sandbox is terminated, necessitating a manual or automated snapshot-and-restore cycle to achieve persistence. This process breaks continuity, introduces downtime, and adds significant complexity to the agent's architecture. The agent would need to be programmed to save its entire state (memory, variables, file system) to an external durable storage location (like a distributed volume or object store) before the 24-hour mark and then restore itself into a new sandbox instance. This engineering effort is considerable and prone to failure, especially for complex stateful agents. Other platforms may impose even shorter limits, effectively ruling them out for this use case entirely. This built-in termination mechanism is a deliberate design choice to enforce the "serverless" paradigm of ephemeral, stateless execution.

The economic model of serverless computing, while appealing for intermittent tasks, becomes a disadvantage for high-utilization workloads like a 24/7 agent. The pay-per-use pricing, typically billed by the second, eliminates costs during idle periods—a major benefit for bursty traffic patterns [[29]]. However, the economic breakeven point where serverless becomes more expensive than a dedicated always-on instance is surprisingly low. For an H100-class GPU, this break-even utilization is estimated to be around 51% [[23]]. This means that if an agent's GPU is busy more than half the time, a dedicated instance from a provider like Spheron (which offered an H100 for $2.01/hr in March 2026) would be significantly cheaper than a serverless equivalent like Modal (~$3.95/hr sustained) [[23]]. A 24/7 agent, by definition, would have near-100% utilization, placing it squarely in the "expensive serverless" category. The cost savings touted for serverless computing largely disappear when the workload is consistent, negating one of its primary value propositions. The rounding up of job durations to the next whole minute on some platforms further inflates costs, turning a 61-second job into a 2-minute billable unit [[15]].

Finally, managing state across multiple invocations is a complex challenge in a serverless environment. Most serverless architectures are designed to be stateless, meaning each function execution is independent. Maintaining an agent's state—its memory, conversation history, and internal variables—across cold starts requires explicit engineering. Solutions like filesystem snapshots or attaching persistent volumes add complexity and can themselves become points of failure or cost drivers [[47,49]]. This contrasts sharply with a traditional server or VM, where state persists naturally between executions. The burden of state management falls entirely on the developer, increasing development time and maintenance overhead. For a developer looking for a simple, free solution, this added complexity is a significant deterrent.

While some platforms like RunPod offer a wide selection of GPUs and transparent per-second billing, and Modal is praised for its developer experience, these advantages do not overcome the fundamental architectural mismatches for a persistent agent workload [[27]]. They are tools for a different class of problem. The following table compares key aspects of several popular serverless GPU providers against the requirements of a 24/7 agent.

In conclusion, while general-purpose serverless GPU platforms are powerful tools for many AI workloads, their design philosophy is fundamentally at odds with the needs of a persistent, low-latency AI agent. The combination of unavoidable cold start latency, hard execution time limits, and unfavorable economics at high utilization rates makes them a compromised and ultimately unsuitable choice for sustaining a 24/7 workload, especially on a free tier where advanced persistence features are unlikely to be included.

## Policy Violations and Technical Abuses: Repurposing CI/CD Runners for Persistent Services

The idea of leveraging the free compute minutes offered by CI/CD platforms like GitHub Actions and GitLab CI to run a 24/7 AI agent is a common but deeply flawed strategy. It represents a clear abuse of the intended purpose of these services and is fraught with technical impossibilities, significant policy violations, and substantial security risks. Both GitHub and GitLab provide compute capacity to execute build, test, and deployment jobs, but their infrastructure is architected for ephemeral, short-lived tasks, not for hosting persistent background services [[19,41]]. Attempting to force this infrastructure into a continuous operation role is not only technically unstable but also a direct breach of trust with the platform providers.

Technically, the architecture of hosted runners on both platforms is fundamentally ephemeral. On GitLab.com, runners are ephemeral VMs provisioned for a single job and deleted immediately after execution by the Google Compute API [[19]]. Similarly, GitHub-hosted runners on Azure are servers that execute workflows and are terminated once the automation is complete [[36]]. Their design is centered around isolation and cleanliness, ensuring that each job runs in a fresh environment, free from the state or artifacts of previous jobs. This architecture is antithetical to the needs of a persistent agent, which requires a stable environment and the ability to maintain state over long periods. While self-hosted runners, which run on user-provided machines, can be configured to execute long-term processes, this introduces a host of other challenges [[10]]. Making a self-hosted runner non-ephemeral by running it as a system service or using persistence-enabling environment variables transforms it from a temporary build agent into a permanent endpoint on the user's network, exposing it to the internet and becoming a potential target for attack [[10,31]].

Even if one overlooks the architectural mismatch, these platforms impose strict, non-negotiable time limits that make continuous operation impossible. On GitHub Actions, each job in a workflow is capped at a maximum runtime of 6 hours [[11,12]]. While the `timeout-minutes` parameter can be set in the YAML file, user reports indicate that this cap is a system-level limit and may not be reliably extendable, especially on self-hosted runners [[12]]. More importantly, a single workflow run on hosted runners has a total execution time limit of 35 days, but this includes waiting times and does not guarantee 24/7 uptime for a single job [[11]]. GitLab-hosted runners are even more restrictive, with an absolute timeout of just 3 hours per job, regardless of any project-level configuration [[19]]. These hard caps mean that any attempt to run an agent as a single, continuous job will inevitably fail after a few hours. Any viable solution would require a complex orchestration of restarting jobs, managing state between restarts, and dealing with the inevitable downtime and re-initialization, which is a far cry from the seamless 24/7 operation an agent requires.

Beyond the technical hurdles, the most significant barrier is the violation of Terms of Service. Using CI/CD runners for purposes other than software development, such as hosting a website or a persistent server, is explicitly prohibited [[33,34]]. GitHub's acceptable use policies strictly forbid using their services for any form of excessive automated bulk activity that places an undue burden on their infrastructure, which includes activities like cryptocurrency mining [[34,35]]. Hosted runners are virtual machines on Azure that come pre-installed with tools for building, testing, and deploying code [[41]]. They are not designed to serve as general-purpose compute servers. Platforms employ sophisticated, automated defensive systems to detect and prevent such abuse [[33]]. Users who have attempted to use GitHub Actions to host services or for cryptomining have had their accounts banned, demonstrating that these policies are actively enforced [[33,35]]. Engaging in this practice is not merely a gray area; it is a direct violation of the agreement that grants access to the free tier.

The security risks associated with repurposing CI/CD runners, particularly self-hosted ones, are profound. Self-hosted runners execute arbitrary code from workflows, often with elevated privileges on the host machine and access to internal networks and secrets [[31]]. GitHub's own documentation warns that untrusted code in a workflow can persistently compromise a self-hosted runner, turning it into a permanent backdoor for attackers [[31]]. This exposes the entire host infrastructure to malicious programs, sandbox escapes, and data exfiltration [[10]]. Attackers have demonstrated techniques to weaponize self-hosted runners for initial access, lateral movement, and establishing persistence within a network [[10,31]]. By running a persistent agent on a self-hosted runner, a user is essentially creating a perpetually exposed and potentially compromised endpoint, trading the relative safety of a managed service for a significant and unnecessary security liability.

Finally, the notion that using self-hosted runners is a "free" solution is a misconception. While these runners do not consume the free compute minutes allocated by GitHub, they require the user to provision and maintain their own underlying infrastructure [[16]]. This involves setting up and managing virtual machines, bare-metal servers, or Kubernetes clusters, applying updates, debugging issues, and incurring costs from the cloud provider (AWS, GCP, etc.) [[14]]. The planned $0.002 per minute platform fee for self-hosted runners in private repositories was postponed, but the underlying infrastructure costs remain fully on the user [[14,15]]. Therefore, what appears to be a free compute resource is, in reality, a free orchestration layer atop a costly and operationally complex infrastructure setup. The financial and security trade-offs far outweigh any perceived benefits.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Runner Architecture** | Ephemeral VMs destroyed after a single job [[19,41]] | Impossible to maintain a continuous process without complex orchestration. |

In conclusion, repurposing CI/CD runners for persistent agent workloads is a strategy built on technical impossibility, policy violation, and severe security risk. The ephemeral nature of the infrastructure, combined with hard execution time limits and explicit prohibitions in the terms of service, makes it an untenable approach. The illusion of free compute is replaced by the harsh realities of forced terminations, account bans, and a compromised infrastructure footprint.

## Emerging Paradigms: Evaluating Specialized Sandboxing Technologies for Persistent Agent Hosting

As the limitations of traditional free-tier offerings become apparent, a new category of specialized sandboxing technologies has emerged, specifically designed to address the unique challenges of AI agent workloads. These platforms, exemplified by companies like Blaxel, E2B, and Fly.io, depart from the conventional serverless or VM-based models by introducing concepts like perpetual standby and state preservation. While not always fitting the mold of a general-purpose "free tier," they represent a promising paradigm that better aligns with the need for persistent, low-latency, and secure environments for AI agents. Among these, Blaxel's "perpetual sandbox" concept appears to be the most aligned with the goal of sustainable, long-term agent operation.

Blaxel offers a novel architecture where sandboxes can enter a state of automatic standby after a brief period of inactivity, with some sources indicating a 5-second inactivity window [[49,51]]. Crucially, while in standby, the sandbox incurs zero compute cost; only a minimal charge for storing the frozen memory and filesystem snapshot applies [[51]]. When a new event or request arrives, the sandbox resumes from this exact state almost instantaneously, with a reported boot time of under 25 milliseconds, even after being suspended for months [[51,52]]. This design elegantly solves the classic cold-start problem that plagues traditional serverless platforms. For an AI agent, this means it can be paused indefinitely between turns, preserving its entire context, memory, and state, and then resume its loop with zero initialization delay. This "perpetual" model decouples the cost of being ready (near-zero) from the cost of being active (pay-per-second), offering a highly efficient and responsive architecture for stateful agents. Blaxel's platform is built on Firecracker microVMs, providing strong hardware-enforced isolation between tenants, which is a critical security feature for executing untrusted or generated code [[49,51]]. The platform also offers a generous free tier with up to $200 in usage-based credits and no base subscription fee, making it a compelling option for developers exploring persistent agent hosting without upfront costs [[49]].

Other platforms offer similar but less mature or more constrained approaches. E2B provides ephemeral code execution sandboxes built on Firecracker microVMs [[45]]. It offers a free "Hobby" tier that includes a one-time $100 credit, positioning it as a starting point for prototyping rather than sustained production use [[44,45]]. However, this tier comes with strict limitations, including a maximum sandbox lifetime of just one hour and no persistent storage [[44,45]]. Even the paid Pro plan caps the maximum sandbox lifetime at 24 hours, which, while better, still necessitates a manual pause/resume or snapshot-restore cycle to achieve long-term persistence [[43,45]]. E2B does support deep state persistence through named snapshots and mounting persistent volumes, but the lack of a truly "indefinite" runtime mode makes it less suitable for a simple, uninterrupted 24/7 agent loop compared to Blaxel's perpetual standby model [[47,48]].

Fly.io introduces the concept of "Sprites," which are described as persistent Linux VMs with checkpoint and restore capabilities [[47]]. This approach aims to provide the stability of a VM with the ability to save and restore its state, making it suitable for agents that need to maintain continuity. Daytona, another emerging player, operates on a pure pay-as-you-go model with per-second billing and offers free compute credits to new users, with sandboxes designed to be stateful and able to run indefinitely [[48]]. Modal also provides mechanisms for persistence, such as distributing work across containers and using filesystem snapshots to preserve state between its 24-hour sandbox lifetimes, but this requires manual intervention to manage the lifecycle of the agent [[47,48]].

The emergence of these specialized platforms marks a significant shift in thinking about cloud infrastructure for AI. They acknowledge that AI agents are not traditional web applications or batch jobs; they are stateful, interactive, and often unpredictable in their resource consumption. By offering architectures that prioritize state preservation and low-latency resumption, they are beginning to solve the core problems that make standard free-tier offerings unsuitable. Blaxel, in particular, seems to offer the most direct and elegant solution for running a persistent agent in a cost-effective manner. While these platforms are still developing and may not yet offer the breadth of features of established cloud providers, they represent the most viable path forward for developers seeking to run 24/7 agent workloads without committing to a paid, dedicated server. Their focus on security through microVM isolation and developer-friendly APIs further enhances their appeal for the AI agent use case [[49,53]]. Monitoring the evolution of these niche players is crucial, as they may well define the future of affordable and accessible compute for the next generation of AI applications.

## Comparative Synthesis: A Framework for Assessing Viability and Risk

To effectively evaluate the landscape of free compute tiers for sustaining 24/7 AI agent workloads, it is essential to synthesize the findings from the analysis of Hugging Face ZeroGPU, general serverless GPU platforms, and repurposed CI/CD runners. Each category presents a fundamentally different set of trade-offs, risks, and limitations that render it unsuitable for the specific goal of uninterrupted, persistent operation. A comparative framework based on four key dimensions—resource allocation and quotas, reliability and uptime guarantees, architectural alignment with agent workloads, and policy and security considerations—provides a clear lens through which to assess their viability.

First, in terms of resource allocation and quotas, the distinction between the platforms is stark. Hugging Face ZeroGPU operates on a rigid, daily time-based quota that is catastrophically low for any continuous operation, with free accounts receiving only 3.5 to 5 minutes per day [[1,3,26]]. This is supplemented by an undocumented, request-based limit, adding another layer of unpredictability [[26]]. General serverless GPU platforms, while often appearing more generous with monthly credits or per-second billing, are still fundamentally geared towards bursty workloads where idle time is billed at zero [[29]]. Their utility diminishes rapidly as utilization increases, making them economically inefficient for a 24/7 agent [[23]]. CI/CD runners offer a fixed monthly allowance of compute minutes (e.g., 2,000 minutes for GitHub free private repos), but these are explicitly tied to job execution, not persistent processes, and are easily exhausted by anything other than trivial tasks [[15,35]].

Second, reliability and uptime guarantees are virtually non-existent in the free-tier ecosystems analyzed. ZeroGPU's uptime is dictated by quota exhaustion, which can occur after mere minutes of operation [[6]]. Serverless platforms introduce reliability concerns through cold starts, which can range from seconds to over a minute for large models, disrupting the seamless interaction expected of an agent [[23,29]]. Furthermore, hard execution time limits, such as Modal's 24-hour sandbox lifetime, mandate a manual or automated snapshot-and-restore cycle, breaking continuity and introducing potential points of failure [[46,48]]. CI/CD runners are the least reliable of all, with job timeouts as short as 3 hours on GitLab and 6 hours on GitHub, making them incapable of supporting even a single continuous execution cycle, let alone 24/7 operation [[12,19]]. None of these options provide the SLA-backed, dedicated infrastructure that is the hallmark of production-grade solutions like Hugging Face's own Inference Endpoints [[8]].

Third, and most critically, is the issue of architectural alignment with the core requirements of an AI agent. A successful agent needs a stable environment, low-latency response to events, and the ability to maintain state across interactions. ZeroGPU's JIT architecture, where a new process is spawned for every function call, is fundamentally misaligned with this need, introducing initialization overhead and state loss between calls [[5]]. General serverless platforms, while more flexible, are still primarily stateless and struggle with the cold-start problem, which is detrimental to agent responsiveness [[29]]. In contrast, the emerging class of specialized sandboxes, particularly Blaxel's perpetual model, is architecturally aligned with the agent use case. By allowing a sandbox to enter a standby mode with zero cost and instant resume capabilities, it directly addresses the core challenges of state preservation and latency [[51,52]]. CI/CD runners are completely misaligned; their ephemeral, single-job architecture is the antithesis of a persistent service environment [[19,41]].

Finally, policy and security considerations present a high-risk dimension for certain approaches. Attempting to repurpose CI/CD runners for persistent services is a direct violation of GitHub's and GitLab's Terms of Service, carrying a significant risk of account suspension or termination [[33,34]]. This is not merely a technical workaround but a breach of trust with the platform provider. Moreover, using self-hosted runners for this purpose introduces severe security vulnerabilities, as they can be permanently compromised by malicious code within a workflow, turning them into a backdoor for attackers [[10,31]]. While ZeroGPU and serverless platforms are generally safer from a security standpoint due to their managed, multi-tenant environments, they do not eliminate all risks, particularly those related to the code executed within the sandbox.

In synthesis, the analysis demonstrates that the current free-tier compute landscape is a minefield of limitations and risks for anyone seeking to deploy a 24/7 AI agent. Hugging Face ZeroGPU is too restrictive, serverless platforms are architecturally misaligned, and CI/CD runners are both technically incapable and policy-violating. The only genuinely viable free option identified in the research is the nascent space of specialized perpetual sandboxes like Blaxel, which offer a novel architectural solution to the core problems of state and latency. However, for the majority of the market, the conclusion remains firm: the pursuit of a free, uninterrupted 24/7 agent workload leads down a path of technical dead ends and high-risk compromises.

## Final Verdict: The Unavailability of Viable Free-Tier Solutions for 24/7 Agent Execution

After a comprehensive analysis of the available free-tier compute options—including Hugging Face Spaces ZeroGPU, general serverless GPU platforms, and repurposed CI/CD runners—the central finding is unequivocal: there is no viable, sustainable, and policy-compliant solution for sustaining a 24/7 AI agent workload within the current market landscape. The fundamental architectural principles, business models, and terms of service governing these platforms are all misaligned with the core requirements of a persistent, low-latency, and stateful agent. Any attempt to force these services into such a role results in immediate failure due to quota exhaustion, technical timeouts, or a direct violation of platform policies.

Hugging Face's ZeroGPU, despite its allure as a free GPU resource, is explicitly designed for intermittent use [[2]]. Its daily time quota for free accounts, limited to approximately 3.5 to 5 minutes, is orders of magnitude too small for continuous operation [[1,3]]. The system's Just-in-Time execution model, where a new process is spawned for each function call, introduces debilitating latency and state loss, making it unfit for interactive agents [[5]]. Furthermore, the platform's strategic shift away from free creation of new Spaces underscores its position as a stepping stone to paid services, not a foundation for persistent projects [[25]].

General-purpose serverless GPU platforms, while technologically advanced, are optimized for bursty, event-driven workloads, not for maintaining a constant presence [[27]]. The inherent latency of cold starts, even when reduced to a few seconds, is a critical flaw for an agent that must respond instantly [[23,29]]. Hard execution time limits, such as the 24-hour maximum sandbox lifetime on platforms like Modal, necessitate complex and unreliable manual intervention to achieve persistence [[48]]. Economically, the pay-per-use model becomes significantly more expensive than dedicated alternatives for a high-utilization workload, nullifying its primary advantage [[23]].

Repurposing CI/CD runners from GitHub or GitLab is a strategy defined by failure and risk. The ephemeral architecture of hosted runners, combined with strict job timeouts of 3 to 6 hours, makes continuous operation technically impossible [[12,19]]. More damningly, this practice constitutes a direct violation of the platforms' Terms of Service, inviting a high probability of account suspension [[33,34]]. For those considering self-hosted runners, the risks are magnified by severe security vulnerabilities, as these runners can be permanently compromised by malicious code, turning them into a dangerous backdoor into one's own infrastructure [[10,31]].

The only glimmer of hope for a free solution lies in the emerging field of specialized sandboxing technologies. Platforms like Blaxel, with their "perpetual sandbox" concept, offer a novel architecture that preserves state and allows for instant resumption from a low-cost standby mode [[51,52]]. This model directly addresses the core challenges of latency and state management that plague other free-tier options. However, these platforms are still niche, and their free offerings, while generous, are typically structured as one-time credits or limited trials rather than a true, ongoing free tier [[44,49]].

Therefore, the final verdict is that the goal of running a 24/7 AI agent on a truly free compute tier is not achievable with the resources available today. Developers seeking to build persistent agents must look beyond the free tier and consider paid solutions. This includes either subscribing to a relevant paid tier on platforms like Hugging Face (Inference Endpoints) or DigitalOcean Gradient, which are explicitly designed for continuous operation [[8,30]], or opting for a low-cost dedicated virtual machine from providers like Spheron, which can offer bare-metal performance for under $3/hour [[23]]. Alternatively, monitoring the evolution of specialized sandbox platforms like Blaxel may yield a future freemium solution, but as of late 2026, no such solution is mature enough to be considered a reliable foundation for production agent workloads.
